## Geometry as constraint

*7/3/2026*

I'm going to walk you through it verbally, because ^^^ all that stuff, is not SAT. The geometry in my head is SAT. Everything else is LLMs trying to turn it into math, which to my mind shouldn't be that hard, it's just that I can't do the math myself. But mathematics--quite frankly--is *loose*. You can do anything with math, and that's why so many theories go off into lala land, representing and representing, without constraining the way the universe constrains. The universe is very obviously geometric (or at least *every* way in which we have to interact with the universe empirically is, in one way or another geometric). Geometry provides its own constraints. Between the rules of geometry, the grammar of the Minkowski diagram, and the inheritance of extremely detailed system behavior from empirical physics, the pieces are in place for it to be put together in a consistent way.

## The worldline as the empirical map

The thing we've been lacking is agreement on a consistent language, lack of cognizance of of what sort of language it would have to be, and failure to recognize one simple fact: Particles are the *only* things we observe. Full stop. Particle worldlines, therefore are the nearest thing we have to a map of all of physics, and if done empirically, a particle worldline is unquestionable because to question it would be to question the accuracy of the physical equations that built it, which we know are accurate to great precision. Put a pin in this.

## Start with what Minkowski already gives you

Now... Minkowski really only gives us two things: The worldline, and the timesheet, and those map, essentially, kinematics, so if you know the worldine, you know the kinematics, and vice-versa. And that gives you a lot of basic physics in your diagram. Now, the question arises: What about things that *aren't* kinematics? Well, gravity basically is kinematics, so we're good there [put a pin in this too]. Electromagnetism? Yes, to some extent, there is kinematics involved--Coulomb repulsion is easy enough to diagram, for example. But what about mass, and spin and flavor and color. Well, could go inventing all sorts of other things to put in the diagram, or we could just put a label on the lines, but that's bad theory-making. Occam tells us that. But it's worth mentioning *why*. The reason is that, for each new element you add, you have not just one new way that you can be wrong--in fact, you open up an entire field of possibilities for going wrong... you can run with math just about anywhere you want to go. But we don't generally want to go chasing pretty math just because you can find some set of equations that seem to fit. Not unless there's a clear and obvious connection that makes physical sense *every step of the way*. Plenty of physics drags us through imaginary nonphysical ideas to get where they're going.

We don't. Or, at least, I don't. The way to proceed is to strictly limit yourself to the elements you have until forced to do otherwise by the logic of the system and empirical findings.

## Representation is easy; constrained representation is the point

So. The elements we have are the worldline and the timesheet. Are those elements sufficient? The answer is unambiguously yes, provably so. A curve has infinite degrees of freedom, therefore a curve has infinite representational power. Obviously, that means it *must* be possible to encode any empirical data, all of the laws of physics, all of the Presidents' birthdays and favorite songs, and anything else you want to encode. A line can do that simply by choosing an arbitrary encoding language for each sort of information. It's easy to prove that to yourself with a variation on linear representation--can Morse code encode everything? Yes, by virtue of the fact that *language* can encode anything.

But arbitrary encoding is not ideal, because you then have to encode every piece of information separately. *Geometrically constrained encoding* is what makes this system work. Minkowski already does this for kinematics and gravity. But that doesn't tell us what sort of encoding makes geometric sense for non-kinematic properties. Unless... we assume everything is kinematic. So... is that doable? Let's think about it. Obviously, a lot of things are clearly kinematic... heat, fluid flow, arguably electrical flow... but again, what about *properties* and *forces*?

### Why this excerpt matters in the lineage

The passage is useful precisely because it separates two claims that are easy to blur: a line can represent arbitrarily much information, but SAT/H(s)H is interested in the much stronger constraint that the information be carried by a small, reusable geometric grammar rather than arbitrary encoding. That distinction becomes increasingly important in the later finite-core/worldtube work.
