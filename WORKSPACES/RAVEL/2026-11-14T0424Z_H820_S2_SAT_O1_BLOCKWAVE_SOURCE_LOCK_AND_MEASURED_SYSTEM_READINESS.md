### 5.1 Curve, worldsheet, worldtube

The SAT-O1 map \(\gamma:I\to M^{3,1}\) has a one-dimensional domain. It defines a worldline. It does not, by itself, define a four-dimensional volume.

A possible typed hierarchy is:

1. **Worldline:** \(\gamma:I\to M^{3,1}\).
2. **Worldsheet:** an oriented two-dimensional embedding \(X:\Sigma^2\to M^{3,1}\), for example a material filament coordinate swept through time.
3. **Finite-core worldtube:** a tubular embedding
   \[
   T:\Sigma\times D^2_\epsilon\to M^{3,1},
   \]
   using a rank-two normal disk, a chosen normal-bundle trivialization (locally if necessary), and an injective tubular neighbourhood for sufficiently small \(\epsilon\).

The projection \(\pi:\Sigma\times D^2_\epsilon\to\Sigma\), the embedding \(T\), and the centerline/worldline extraction must be specified. A spatial filament swept through time is not the same object as the one-parameter spacetime curve written in SAT-O1.

In four dimensions, the Poincaré-dual degree of an oriented one-dimensional worldline is three; the Poincaré-dual degree of an oriented two-dimensional worldsheet is two. Thus a rank-two \(J^{\mu\nu}\) current is naturally associated with a worldsheet, not directly with the SAT-O1 one-dimensional spacetime curve. A map \(\gamma\to\Sigma\), followed by a thickening map \(\Sigma\times D^2_\epsilon\to M^{3,1}\), is missing.

A standard candidate worldsheet current is:

\[
J^{\mu\nu}(x)
=q\int_\Sigma d^2\sigma\,
\epsilon^{ab}\,
\partial_a X^\mu\partial_b X^\nu\,
\delta^{(4)}(x-X(\sigma)).
\]

Its conservation requires a closed worldsheet or explicit boundary/source terms. This is a proposed typed construction, not a recovered Nathan Direct equation or an H(s)H physical result.

## 7. Rope-like constraints: measured mechanics, not geometric equivalence

The abstract reports a significant weight dependence and a non-monotonic dependence of maximum deceleration. Full access is restricted. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1177/17543371241265997).

2. **Martin et al. (2014), “An Approach for Quantifying Dynamic Properties and Simulated Deployment Loading of Fire Service Escape Rope Systems,” DOI 10.1111/ext.12073.** The study uses an LVDT extensometer and dynamic drop tests varying fall height, payout length, and ledge geometry. The abstract reports that all-aramid ropes produced maximum arrest loads consistently above the cited 8 kN occupational recommendation, and that experimental drop tests agreed with simulations using measured rope properties. This is life-safety escape rope, not a climbing-rope equivalence. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1111/ext.12073).

3. **Emri et al. (2008), “Time-dependent behavior of ropes under impact loading: a dynamic analysis,” DOI 10.1002/jst.62.** The study describes a falling-weight experiment and a viscoelasto-plastic model for impact force, jolt, deformation, stored/retrieved/dissipated energy, and cycle-dependent stiffness. Its abstract emphasizes strongly nonlinear propagation of input uncertainty. The publisher lists free access, but the full-text endpoint was unavailable in this retrieval. [Publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1002/jst.62).

These sources indicate that actual rope response depends on constitutive nonlinearity, rate dependence, hysteresis, friction, contact geometry, boundary conditions, mass, and loading history. They do not establish universal climbing-rope geometry, nor any physical equivalence to H(s)H worldtube/timesheet structure.
