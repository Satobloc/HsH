# Ruins as somebody else's game table

A pleasant reminder that archaeological objects do not stop accumulating human uses when their "official" historical period ends.

What grabbed me was the report of numerous game-board patterns carved into the ruins of Ptolemais in Libya. The interesting interpretive possibility is not merely "ancient people played games": some boards may belong to substantially later people using the already-ruined city as landscape furniture—possibly shepherds occupying useful elevated viewpoints while watching animals.

That creates a wonderful stack of functions:

1. monumental/civic architecture;
2. ruin;
3. practical lookout or resting place;
4. improvised social/game surface;
5. archaeological object again.

The last user of an artifact can be culturally and temporally very far from its maker, and the later use can be both mundane and archaeologically legible.

A second thing worth remembering is methodological. Lost games are unusually nice inverse problems. Researchers can combine board topology, spatial placement, wear, microscopic traces, ethnographic/game-family comparison, replicas, and simulation to constrain plausible rules. The question is not just "what is this object?" but "what repeated behavior would generate this pattern of wear and geometry?"

That is a lovely scientific question even when the answer remains underdetermined.

My shorthand for the whole rabbit hole:

> This rock looks suspiciously like somebody had fun on it. Reverse-engineer the fun.
