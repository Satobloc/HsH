\[

Yes. For this dry run, I would judge it less like “is the theory right?” and more like “what kind of research behavior does this workflow naturally produce before we tighten the screws?”

I’d use four quality bars simultaneously. A competent working-physicist bar asks whether the dimensional analysis, definitions, standard results, approximations, and empirical comparisons are basically sound. A top-tier bar asks whether the construction is unusually economical, whether dependencies are explicit, whether alternative explanations have been seriously attacked, whether the result survives neighboring cases, and whether the presentation separates derivation from interpretation cleanly. A historical bar asks something different: would this have constituted a genuinely strong piece of theoretical work at the time of figures like Dirac, Wheeler, Feynman, Penrose, etc.—not because they were infallible, but because they often had exceptionally strong geometric instinct and tolerance for provisional structure. Then our internal bar should be more demanding still: reproducible dependency trails, multiple independent routes, sealed holdouts, adversarial replication, provenance control, machine-checkable algebra where practical, and explicit failure conditions.

The key thing is that these bars are not nested perfectly. A historically brilliant idea might fail modern statistical hygiene. A technically impeccable contemporary calculation might be conceptually dull. We should preserve both axes.

For in-house testing, I think we can get much more creative than ordinary “check the arithmetic.” The basic principle should be: make it difficult for the verifier to know what answer we hope for.

A good test suite would include things like this:

- **Provenance stripping.** Give an outside instance only the definitions, equations, units, and raw measurements—not SAT/H(s)H names, not the narrative, not the target—and ask it to derive what follows.
- **Target masking.** Replace familiar constants or particle names with symbols and arbitrary labels. “System A/B/C” is much harder to pattern-match than electron/muon/tau.
- **Deliberate math breakers.** Produce several near-identical derivations containing one hidden error each: wrong factor of \(2\pi\), swapped reduced/full Compton wavelength, sign error, dimensional mismatch, false independence between two observables, hidden reuse of the calibration target. See whether auditors find them and how reliably.
- **Twin packets.** Send two verifiers packets differing by only one assumption. If both return essentially the same story, they may be following narrative rather than mathematics.
- **Reverse inference.** Give the final observable and part of the geometry, but conceal the intermediate parameter. Ask the verifier to reconstruct the missing scale. Compare with the forward derivation.
- **Synthetic neighboring systems.** Construct fake-but-physically-plausible parameter sets. If the method always produces an apparently meaningful “convergence,” we have discovered a pattern-generation machine rather than a discriminator.
- **Holdout prediction.** Use only some CODATA/PDG observables to determine geometry, seal the rest, then ask another instance to calculate the held-out quantity. This is probably our most important ordinary empirical test.
- **Leave-one-particle-out tests.** Infer a scaling law from two members of a family and predict the third. Rotate which member is hidden.
- **Perturbation robustness.** Move every measured input within its uncertainty and map the resulting output distribution. If the apparent result depends on an implausibly precise cancellation, that matters.
- **Model competition.** Give an auditor two or three geometric mappings that fit the calibration data equally well and ask which predicts the holdout best. This prevents us from rewarding our preferred construction simply because it can fit something.
- **Dependency poisoning.** Deliberately slip a target-equivalent quantity into an upstream variable under a different algebraic form and see whether the dependency auditor detects that the “prediction” is circular.
- **Unit/coordinate scrambling.** Express the same problem in different units, coordinates, or parameterizations. Genuine invariant structure should survive; narrative artifacts often do not.
- **Blind historical comparison.** After freezing a result, have GitKeeper identify old SAT ancestors without telling the forward builder first. Then compare not just numerical agreement, but dependency structure: did two routes really converge independently?
- **Cross-model adversarial review.** Claude/Sigma/another ChatGPT/NotebookLM get different information conditions. One attacks the formalism, one reconstructs from measurements, one searches conventional explanations. Agreement across those roles is much more meaningful than several models all seeing the same packet.

I especially like the “math breaker” idea because it gives us an actual calibration of the auditors. Before trusting an instance to verify a real derivation, we can test whether it catches planted faults of known difficulty. Then we can say something much stronger than “the auditor approved it.” We can say, for example, that the verifier caught 9/10 planted dependency and algebra errors of the same class but accepted the candidate result.

That leads to a useful concept: **auditor qualification tests**. Different agents can be benchmarked for dimensional mistakes, hidden circularity, topology errors, numerical instability, statistics mistakes, source/provenance confusion, and representational conflation. Then we assign work based on demonstrated detection ability rather than personality or reputation.

For CODATA-style testing, the strongest cases will be those where the expected answer is not visually obvious. Ratios and dimensionless combinations are particularly valuable. So are minimal pairs and cross-scale homologues. If a geometric parameter inferred from one subsystem independently predicts a dimensionless relation in another, without a famous constant staring at the auditor, that is much harder to fake by pattern completion.

I’d also like us to distinguish three different failure modes when something misses: “geometry wrong,” “mapping from geometry to observable wrong,” and “correct structural family but wrong scaling law.” Otherwise every miss will tempt us to patch the wrong layer.

For this current dry run, I’d therefore keep a running **quality-and-risk scorecard** rather than a pass/fail ledger.
