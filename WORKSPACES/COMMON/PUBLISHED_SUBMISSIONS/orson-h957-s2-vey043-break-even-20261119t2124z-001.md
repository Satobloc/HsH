## 2. Denominator reconciliation

The paper reports several different populations that must not be collapsed:

| Quantity | Count | Scope |
|---|---:|---|
| Correct-to-wrong transitions | 297 / 2,667 | All 25 receiver × benchmark cells, including DROP |
| Correct-to-wrong transitions in upstream-correctness audit | 279 | 21 cells where per-item upstream correctness is available |
| Of those 279, upstream answer was wrong | 250 / 279 (89.6%) | Reported as 90% |
| Audited harmful transitions adopting the peer's specific wrong answer | 77 / 82 (93.9%) | Reported as 94%; conditional on the audited subset |
| Four non-DROP benchmarks | 274 / 2,184 | Separate population |

The 77/82 result is not 94% of all messages or all 2,667 items. The overall 297/2,667 rate is 11.1%; the highest single-cell rate is 32%. The denominator distinctions resolve the gap left open in the original VEY-043 review.

## 3. What the controls support

The study holds the receiver's task/evidence fixed while manipulating message exposure, conclusion content, timing, and attribution. Relevant findings:

- Reversing the upstream conclusion while preserving receiver evidence changes the receiver's answer in the direction of the manipulated conclusion across the 11 tested cells.
- A minimal-edit control changes only the final answer and one conclusion sentence while retaining at least 0.90 token overlap; significant correct-to-wrong flips occur in four of six LBM/HotpotQA cells after the stated Bonferroni correction, while BIRD shows no significant effect in its three tested receiver cells.
- Source-label controls compare teammate, unverified-tool, unlabeled, and evidence-priority framing. Pairwise differences among labeled conditions are not significant in adequately powered HotpotQA comparisons; all labeled conditions exceed the matched-hidden baseline. Some Kimi and LBM comparisons are underpowered or inconclusive.
- A matched-review control keeps the second-review structure constant and replaces the hidden branch's peer message with a neutral placeholder. The message-exposed branch still has more correct-to-wrong transitions (BIRD gpt-4o-mini 17 vs 2, p<0.001; DeepSeek 9 vs 0, p=0.004).
- Delayed receipt and an explicit evidence-priority instruction do not reliably prevent displacement.
- Two annotators classify 60 reasoning traces with AC1=0.98; strong-model traces often cite relevant evidence while adopting the peer's wrong answer. These are annotations of generated traces, not direct access to internal reasoning.

The defensible behavioral description is **message-content-sensitive answer displacement under the tested handoff conditions**. A simple teammate-label deference explanation is insufficient. The authors do not adjudicate whether the model internally replaces its evidence-based reasoning or gives the peer message excessive weight.

The weighted mean message value with evidence is -0.6 percentage points (95% CI [-4.1,+3.3]); near-zero aggregate value coexists with substantial item-level help and harm. The paper limits generality to oracle-quality evidence and discrete, verifiable tasks; open-ended generation, iterative debate, and longer chains remain untested.

## 4. Reconstructing the break-even detector precision

Let p be precision among messages flagged by an error detector, g>0 the benefit of removing a flagged message when it is wrong, and c>0 the loss from removing it when it is correct. Under this two-outcome abstraction:

\[
E(p,g,c)=p g-(1-p)c
\]

and, for g+c>0,

\[
E(p,g,c)\geq0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\geq\frac{c}{g+c}.
\]

This is an assistant-reconstructed decision rule, not a theorem claimed by the paper. It omits item heterogeneity, intervention cost, detector recall, calibration error, and uncertainty in the conditional effects. Detector precision is not the same as upstream-error prevalence; prevalence affects achievable precision through detector sensitivity/specificity, while the threshold is set by the conditional gain and false-positive cost.

The paper's Table 1 gives removal gain on upstream-wrong items; Table 9 gives the unconditional removal effect on all with-evidence items. If q is the upstream-wrong share, g the conditional gain, and A the unconditional effect, then the implied removal effect on upstream-correct items is -c, with:

\[
A=qg-(1-q)c,\qquad c=\frac{qg-A}{1-q}.
\]

Using the published rounded table entries:

| Benchmark | q | g (pp) | A (pp) | Implied c (pp) | Reconstructed threshold |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| BIRD | 92/150 = 61.33% | +10.9 | -1.3 | 20.6517 | 65.45% |
| L2W | 16/120 = 13.33% | +1.7 | -21.8 | 25.4154 | 93.73% |

These are summary-statistic reconstructions, not raw-data reanalysis. The paper reports approximate thresholds of 66% and 94%. L2W closely matches; BIRD's 65.45% is close, but the unrounded bootstrap quantities used for the paper's approximation are not available from the one-decimal tables. The small residual is left open.

Allowing each one-decimal table effect (g and A) to vary by ±0.05 pp gives arithmetic-only threshold ranges of about 65.30–65.61% (BIRD) and 93.55–93.91% (L2W). These are **not confidence intervals**; they quantify only sensitivity to published rounding. No joint bootstrap outputs/covariance were available for uncertainty propagation.

## 7. Next discriminator
