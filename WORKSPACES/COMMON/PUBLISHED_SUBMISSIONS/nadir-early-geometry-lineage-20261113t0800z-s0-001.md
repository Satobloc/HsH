## 2. Nathan Direct geometry, with the later mathematics bracketed

### 2.1 The moving-plane toy

The transcript's direct geometry proceeds in a clear sequence:

1. A straight line supplies a long axis.
2. A helical line wraps around that axis.
3. A plane perpendicular to the straight line intersects each ideal one-dimensional line at one point, named S and H.
4. For one fixed plane, there are two points, not a spiral of intersections within the plane.
5. As the plane moves while preserving perpendicularity to the axis, S is stationary in the plane-local two-dimensional readout and H traces a circle around S.
6. The orbit radius is the distance from the axis to the helix, equal to the helix's cross-sectional radius. It is not set by pitch.
7. Pitch and plane speed determine the rate at which H traverses the circle.

The assistant's first response incorrectly described a spiral pattern of intersections in one fixed plane. Nathan corrected it immediately. The assistant later repeated the pitch/radius confusion; Nathan corrected that too.

### 2.2 Other line shapes and plane readouts

The direct conversation then adds:

- A line crossing a moving plane obliquely produces a straight trace in the plane.
- With an explicit x/y plane and z-directed plane motion, a line constrained perpendicular to x can produce a y-directed trace.
- Tilting the axis changes the direction of the trace.
- A perfect helix retains constant orbit radius; it cannot make its two intersection points converge merely by selecting a different axial slice.
- A pair of oppositely opening parabolas is proposed to make two plane-intersection points approach, meet, and separate.

The parabolic proposal does not fully specify the direction of plane motion in its own sentence. The assistant silently chooses negative y, although the preceding coordinate setup used z-directed plane motion. That direction is not promoted here.

The resulting shape-to-motion vocabulary is explicitly heuristic: straight/perpendicular structures yield static points; oblique lines yield uniform traces; curved lines can yield accelerated traces; wavy lines can yield oscillatory traces; helices yield circular/orbital readouts; and multiple intersections can produce apparent merging or duplication. This is a descriptive vocabulary, not a theorem that every arbitrary motion has a unique line-shape representation.

### 2.3 The early 4D lift and its semantic boundary

Nathan proposes lifting the picture one dimension: the shapes and combinations of lines extend through four dimensions, while a three-dimensional time surface intersects them. The intersection points are called particles, and the extended structures are called strings for lack of a better word.

The transcript then makes an important qualification. Nathan first distinguishes this construction from what string theory ordinarily means by strings, then says there is some overlap and that his intuition was partly inspired by an early, vague understanding of string theory. The faithful classification is therefore:

- the early SAT strings are project-specific geometric structures;
- string theory is an acknowledged inspiration/comparison;
- identity with standard string-theory strings is not established;
- later GR and ST language must not be mapped automatically onto the early filament vocabulary.

The user also locates complexity primarily in interactions among the four-dimensional structures. Their intersections with the time surface are the three-dimensional manifestation of those structures, not the asserted source of all their complexity. This early emphasis is compatible with a readout role for the surface, but does not by itself define whether the later timesheet also has its own dynamics.

The relevant conditional dimension identities in a four-dimensional ambient space are:

\[
1+3-4=0,\qquad 2+3-4=1,\qquad 4+3-4=3.
\]

Thus a one-dimensional filament intersecting a three-dimensional hypersurface generically gives a point; a two-dimensional worldsheet gives a one-dimensional trace; and a four-dimensional support region gives a three-dimensional slice. These are conditional dimension statements, not proof that the source's different uses of filament, worldtube, or current denote the same object.

A hierarchy requires explicit maps. In particular, SAT-O1's 1D curve does not become a 4D finite-core worldtube merely by naming the swept object. A map such as E: gamma x K3 -> M4, with a defined cross-section K3, embedding, and boundary conditions, would be needed. No such source-defined map is supplied by the SAT-O1 text.

\[

\left(\lambda,
A_x\sin(k_x\lambda+\phi_x),
A_y\sin(k_y\lambda+\phi_y),
A_z\sin(k_z\lambda+\phi_z)\right),
\qquad
v^\mu(\lambda)=\frac{d\gamma^\mu}{d\lambda}.

It then gives:

\[
S[\gamma]=\int d\lambda\,L(\gamma^\mu,v^\mu),

It describes the worldtube as the 4D volume swept out by the vibrating filament. The sweep, thickening, or cross-section map is not defined in the file.
