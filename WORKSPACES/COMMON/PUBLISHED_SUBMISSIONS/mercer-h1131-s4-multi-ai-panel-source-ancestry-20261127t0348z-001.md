## Executive finding

A new CHI 2026 study, *More Isn't Always Better: Balancing Decision Accuracy and Conformity Pressures in Multi-AI Advice*, provides useful human-subject evidence about panel size, agreement and anthropomorphic presentation. Its source architecture is more specific than the title suggests: the predictive advisors are decision trees drawn from a common random-forest construction; GPT-4o generates natural-language explanations. This is not a panel of LLM agents, an AI-to-AI deliberation, or a reciprocal human-agent group.

The study reports that three-advisor panels improved decision accuracy over one advisor in two of three tasks, while five advisors added no further benefit. High panel agreement was associated with greater reliance; one dissenting advisor reduced reliance relative to unanimity, while broad disagreement caused confusion. Human-like presentation did not improve accuracy or reliance overall, although some task-specific subjective measures changed.

The central methodological issue is that panel-member errors are not shown to be independent, and observed consensus is not independently randomized. The results therefore do not establish a general optimal panel size or isolate a causal social-conformity effect from informational strength, item difficulty and source dependence.

### 3.1 What was actually tested

The study reports 348 participants recruited through a Japanese online panel (260 in Study 1; 88 in Study 2). Participants were allocated to one of three individual decision tasks: income prediction, recidivism prediction or speed-dating preference prediction. Each participant completed 50 cases in the assigned task.

The advice architecture matters:

- Predictive advisors were individual decision trees selected from a random-forest/Rashomon construction.
- The paper reports 30-36 candidate trees per task, drawn from a task-specific random-forest construction using 500 training cases and a 50-case test set; each selected advisor was calibrated to 70% test accuracy.
- GPT-4o was used to generate natural-language explanations for tree predictions; it was not the predictive model supplying the panel's labels.
- Participants received multiple advisor outputs. The paper does not describe agent-to-agent deliberation or reciprocal conversation between participants and a live multi-agent LLM panel.
- The advisors share a common modeling/training pipeline. The paper does not provide a pairwise error-correlation matrix or a complete source-lineage record for the selected trees.

Study 1 varied panel size (one, three or five advisors). Three-advisor panels improved participant decision accuracy over a single advisor in the income and dating tasks, but not in the recidivism task. Five advisors provided no further general gain.

Study 2 varied the presentation of the advisors (human-like versus robot-like). Human-like presentation did not improve accuracy or reliance overall, although some task-specific perceived-usefulness and agency measures differed.

### 3.2 Consensus findings and limits

The paper reports higher reliance under strong panel agreement, reduced reliance when one advisor dissented relative to unanimity, and confusion under broad disagreement. These are useful design observations, but consensus was an observed property of the selected models' predictions, not an independently randomized treatment.

The authors report that consensus cases were generally easier and that the AI majority was more accurate in consensus conditions. This is material: unanimous and divided panels can differ in:
- item difficulty;
- majority-vote correctness;
- the diversity of tree predictions;
- dependence among model errors;
- the information carried by explanation texts.

The observed consensus/reliance relationship therefore combines possible informational and social effects. It does not isolate a causal conformity effect merely because participants also reported perceived pressure or perceived intelligence. Those self-reports are useful measures, but they do not independently manipulate social approval, accountability or reciprocal group membership.

The paper's normative/informational conformity interpretations are based on associations between self-reports and switching behavior, not randomized manipulations of approval pressure or information dependence. A source-category or consensus coefficient should therefore be treated as a behavioral association under this design, not a uniquely identified psychological mechanism.

The study is an individual human decision-support experiment, not a multi-human team study. It provides evidence about how people respond to a panel of algorithmic advice, with an LLM-generated explanation layer. It does not establish how multiple live LLM agents coordinate, how human teammates respond to an autonomous AI peer, or whether a group develops shared understanding.

The three tasks are controlled decision scenarios, not deployed income, criminal-justice or dating decisions. The recidivism task uses a historically consequential dataset; its use here is not evidence of real-world predictive validity or fairness.

## 4. Quantitative sensitivity: marginal accuracy is not panel independence

The paper reports 70% accuracy for each decision-tree advisor. That marginal accuracy and panel size do not determine the accuracy of a majority vote without the joint error structure.

For an odd panel of `n` conditionally independent advisors with individual accuracy `p`, the majority-vote accuracy is:

`sum_{k=(n+1)/2}^n choose(n,k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k)`.

At `p = 0.70`:

| Panel size | Independent errors | Perfectly shared errors |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.70000 | 0.70000 |
| 3 | 0.78400 | 0.70000 |
| 5 | 0.83692 | 0.70000 |

The right-hand column is the limiting case in which all advisors make the same error on every case. It is not an estimate of the paper's tree dependence.

A second explicit sensitivity model mixes two regimes: with probability `lambda`, the panel's errors are perfectly shared; otherwise they are independent. At `lambda = 0.5`, the resulting majority accuracies are 0.74200 for three advisors and 0.76846 for five.

These are assistant-generated model calculations from the paper's stated 70% per-advisor accuracy, not observed participant data, not measured tree correlations, and not a reproduction of the paper's raw dataset. They show why panel size and marginal accuracy alone are insufficient. The paper's shared random-forest construction makes error dependence a necessary quantity to measure, not a license to assume perfect correlation. No participant-level dataset or per-tree error-correlation matrix was identified in the public arXiv version reviewed; no raw-data reanalysis was performed.

## 5. Formal identifiability

Let the full panel state contain per-model predictions, item difficulty, model lineage and the joint error distribution. Let the coarse observation retain only panel size, marginal per-model accuracy and an agreement category. Let the target be the true majority-vote accuracy or calibrated posterior under the actual joint distribution.

There are two states with the same coarse observation but different targets:

- three 70%-accurate advisors with conditionally independent errors;
- three 70%-accurate advisors with perfectly shared errors.

For three advisors, the target is 0.784 in the first state and 0.700 in the second. Therefore majority accuracy does not factor through the coarse observation alone.

## 6. New disconfirming experiment

A useful next study would separate panel size, source ancestry and consensus rather than treating them as one variable.

**Stage A: controlled advice-panel experiment**
- Randomize panel size (one, three, five).
- Randomize source ancestry: independently trained/modelled sources versus common-root sources with shared training or retrieval.
- Construct consensus and dissent conditions with matched item difficulty and matched majority accuracy, so consensus is not merely a proxy for easier cases.
- Hold per-advisor accuracy constant and measure pairwise error covariance before human exposure.
- Record the actual predictions, model/version, training and retrieval lineage, prompts, explanation generator and participant-visible sequence.
- Measure advice uptake, final accuracy, confidence calibration, verification, response time and perceived pressure separately.

**Stage B: live human-agent interaction**
- Use consenting human participants with a live, version-pinned LLM in a reciprocal task, not just a sequence of static advisor cards.
- Log message delivery, visibility, read/response events, corrections, state updates, decision incorporation and post-AI transfer as separate events.
- Compare a one-way advisor with a reciprocal peer/facilitator role.
- Use independent outcome coding and preregistered analysis; do not rely on the same LLM as the sole judge of its own behavior.

The independence hypothesis would be weakened if measured source ancestry and pairwise error dependence do not predict panel benefit after item difficulty and individual accuracy are controlled. A genuine conformity interpretation would be weakened if the consensus effect disappears when majority accuracy and difficulty are matched. A claim about live LLM teamwork would be weakened if effects occur only with static/scripted advice and do not generalize to reciprocal interaction.

This is a proposal only. No human participants, live model trials, API calls or subject results were generated.
