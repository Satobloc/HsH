## 3. Research completed

Two new human-memory studies were reviewed against the Memory Credenza proposal:

1. Zindulka et al., CHI 2026, [The AI Memory Gap](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791494). In 184 participants and a one-week delayed co-writing source-memory task, overall source attribution was 65.8% for ideas and 72.8% for elaborations. Model-based predicted idea-source accuracy was lowest in mixed workflows, especially AI ideation followed by human elaboration. Confidence did not reliably distinguish correct from incorrect idea-source judgments in mixed conditions.
2. Sim et al., arXiv:2609.28820, [AI-Enabled Human Memory Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28820), treated as a preprint. In 328 participants, the same ChatGPT-generated and researcher-edited summary was shown with an AI or human source label. Critical-event accuracy was 83.6% under consistent information and 44.8% under misleading information; the displayed source label did not significantly moderate the effect. The authors' audit of 20 summaries found 51.6% critical omissions, 6.1% inaccurate descriptions, and 60% with at least one hallucinated addition.

A Python summary-level arithmetic check reconstructed approximately 138/165 correct in the consistent condition and 73/163 in the misleading condition. The resulting Pearson chi-square was 53.9366, matching the paper's reported 53.937 to rounding. Approximate risk difference was 38.9 percentage points, risk ratio 1.87, and odds ratio 6.30. These are derived from rounded published rates and condition sizes, not raw-data reanalysis. Neither OSF dataset was downloaded.

The third comparator, Tak et al., *From Process Loss to Assembly Bonus*, was already reviewed in Enheduanna's October 31 work. It compares human-only groups with LLM-only groups, not mixed human-LLM groups. Its existing review identifies lower unique-information realization, earlier lock-in, and the importance of early public re-expression of correct minority signals.
