## 1. Operating aim and model-selection preference

H(s)H/SAT aims to construct the minimal model consistent with observations and with the chosen, revisable Minkowski geometric representational grammar, while making the model as graspable as possible through human geometric and causal intuitions.

There are indefinitely many, and in general unboundedly many, models that may be effective with respect to a given body of observations. Predictive adequacy alone therefore does not determine which model we choose to develop. We deliberately prefer models that make the most effective use of human geometric and physical intuitions: models whose structure, mechanisms, and relationships can be grasped, inspected, reasoned about, and extended by human thinkers.

This is a methodological preference, not an ontological requirement or a claim that the most intuitive model must be true. More complex models may be equally effective or preferable under other criteria. We choose to impose the additional discipline of human intelligibility because an explanatory model that can be understood and worked with is a valued goal of this project.

Model choice therefore considers both:
- **Adequacy:** how effectively the model organizes the apparent regularities of the available observation space and how well its predictions agree with observations.
- **Intelligibility:** how effectively the model uses human geometric and causal intuitions to make those regularities understandable.

These aims can reinforce one another, but they are not identical. Intelligibility is a deliberate preference, not evidence of empirical adequacy.


## 2. Minimal geometric model and primitive discipline

The aim is to construct the minimal model consistent with observations and with the chosen, revisable Minkowski geometric representational grammar, while making the model as graspable as possible through human geometric and causal intuitions.

### 2.1 Primitive starting point

The minimal starting model has two primitives:

1. **Worldline/worldtube:** the physical structural primitive, with worldtube now preferred as the general representation where finite spatial extent and internal structure matter.
2. **Timesheet:** the complementary geometric structure.

The exact roles, mutual relations, and mathematical types of these primitives are to be explicitly specified, not inferred from their names.

A **responsive/reactive medium** is provisionally adopted as a third primitive for the purpose of continuing the investigation. This is a working device, not an established ontological commitment. The explicit test is whether the medium:
- can be eliminated and represented by the two-primitive worldtube–timesheet model;
- can be absorbed into an elaborated or restructured timesheet; or
- must remain an independent third primitive, but only if evidence and prior geometric commitments require it.

Modeling with the third primitive is currently useful to move forward, while the two-primitive alternatives remain the minimal targets of comparison.

### 2.2 Complexity admission rule

Avoid adding complication until geometry or evidence requires it to improve consistency with observations or internal geometric coherence. When a new structure seems necessary:
1. First test whether a modification of an existing primitive can represent the phenomenon.
2. Prefer a modification that is geometrically likely, and ideally geometrically necessary, over an ad hoc addition.
3. Examine the phenomenon across multiple systems, preferably systems otherwise well understood.
4. If no resolution is forced, and another part of the theory can be developed without adding complexity, pin the unresolved question and continue elsewhere.
5. Revisit the question when developments elsewhere constrain it.

The desired result is not the most elaborate model. It is the most economical, internally coherent and human-graspable geometric model that remains consistent with the evidence.

## 4. Core construction and investigation methods

### 4.1 Star-Shaped Derivation Map

The Star-Shaped Derivation Map is also referred to as:
- Loop Derivation Torquing;
- Back Hallways Approach;
- Zero-Parameter Dimensionless Model with Single-Constant Anchoring.

Use it as a cross-sector constraint and derivation discipline. A correction to one spoke must be propagated through the connected derivation structure, including failed predictions; do not tune one spoke independently or quietly discard inherited mechanisms.

### 4.2 Fractal cross-scale coarse-graining

Use cross-scale comparison while explicitly accounting for measured scale-dependent differences, including:
- proportional spacing, size, and geometry;
- flexibility differences between worldtubes/worldlines;
- known scale-dependent response.

Where useful, construct a counterfactual scaled system: for example, ask what would be required to construct a planet that behaves like an electron. Do not assume scale similarity where measured or established differences contradict it.

The current geometric intuition is that Minkowski worldtube curvature is proportional to acceleration, so rigidity of a given body may be calculable from a standard-physics-based 4D map. Treat this as a claim to formalize and test, with definitions and domain limits, rather than an unqualified theorem.

### 4.3 Map → examine → identify

The detailed procedure is:

1. **Select the physical system.** Identify systems in which the target phenomenon is present. Prefer systems well characterized by standard models, mappable to Minkowski space using standard covariant equations, with reliable measured values (including CODATA where relevant). Prefer cases where the phenomenon is constrained by better-understood phenomena.
2. **Build the 4D map.** Map every known part of the system in four dimensions using the current Minkowski geometric grammar and cumulative geometric commitments. Use as much known detail as possible and conform to standard physics and measured values as closely as available data permit. This is a map of established knowledge as far as it goes, not proof of the proposed interpretation.
3. **Find minimal pairs.** Wherever possible, compare systems that differ chiefly in the presence or absence of the phenomenon under investigation.
4. **Set aside standard labels.** Closely inspect the system topology and how its 4D morphology corresponds to well-understood behavior. Keep the working intuitions in view: motion is angle, acceleration is curvature, inertia is rigidity. Ask: **WWRD?**
5. **Locate geometric correlates and possible transmission channels.** Identify candidate geometric correlates and possible local force-transmission channels by following the w-extent of all involved worldtubes. Introduce interactions or comparator configurations when they can expose otherwise hidden geometry.
6. **Narrow the location and form.** Use the known 4D geometry, current primitives, prior geometric commitments, standard physics and existing SAT hypotheses to narrow where the phenomenon can reside and what geometric structures could represent it.
7. **Add comparators when needed.** Seek systems whose geometry constrains the candidate interpretation further or differently.
8. **Rank possibilities conservatively.** Restrict the possible placements and structures. Distinguish those within existing commitments from those requiring extension, ordered from least disruptive to most disruptive.
9. **Calculate alternatives.** Where permitted and supported by genuine data, calculate under different geometric assumptions to exclude or promote candidates. Respect current real-data-first and data-free-calculation permission gates.
10. **Select or retain alternatives.** Choose the best-supported geometry or a small set of equivalent/alternative structures, explicitly distinguishing mathematical equivalence from genuinely different hypotheses.
11. **Pin unresolved cases.** If no clear decision emerges, preserve the uncertainty and move to another productive part of the theory.

In practice, a plausible geometric solution often becomes apparent quickly once a well-mapped system is inspected. That practical observation is not permission to skip source recovery, calculations, formalization, or hostile tests when the claim requires them.

## 9. Adaptive LLM workflow, worker development and lane specialization

This section generalizes the experimental workflow principles being developed in **The Big Push**. It is an operational direction for active experimentation, not a claim that the full institutional architecture or all supporting automations have been implemented.

### 9.1 Repository-backed personal capability scaffolding

For extended work, each worker/thread should develop and maintain a compact, purpose-specific personal operating capsule: a small, frequently consulted executive-function and continuity reference that keeps mission, role, current priorities, operating principles, next actions and critical pointers readily available. The capsule should remain small enough to be useful within the active context and should point downward to progressively more detailed, selectively loaded materials.

The intended hierarchy is:

1. **Executive capsule:** role, mission, current intentions, priorities, current work state, governing constraints, load order, and pointers to the next layer.
2. **Working protocol and capability shelf:** compact second-tier references describing available tools, repositories, content, scripts, search and excavation methods, workflow patterns, reminders, known pitfalls, useful habits, and current plans.
3. **Purpose-specific modules:** detailed skill and knowledge documents, tool instructions, source maps, notes, scripts, checklists, calendars, audit methods, and project-specific procedures.
4. **Primary resources and implementation:** source material, code, datasets, tool interfaces, and evidence needed for the current operation.

Every layer should link both downward to further detail and upward to the capsule and its current control state. The capsule is not a static prompt or a dumping ground: it is a living, frequently revisited point of return. Update it as roles, projects, capabilities, priorities and effective methods change. Use progressive disclosure so that only the material needed for the current operation enters the active context.

### 9.2 Adaptive self-management and attention cycling

The hierarchy is intended to support a recurring zoom-in / zoom-out loop:
- Start from the executive capsule, recover the current purpose and priority, and select one bounded operation.
- Load only the relevant detailed references and tools, then carry out the operation with inspectable evidence.
- Periodically zoom back out to reassess the wider plan, neglected branches, dependencies, available alternatives, scope and priorities.
- Review the capsule and lower-level modules for stale procedures, omissions, friction, successful innovations and useful new capabilities.
- Refresh the next action and return pointer, then resume at the appropriate level.

Purpose-built reminders, calendars, alarm/heartbeat mechanisms, scripts and occasional notes-to-self may help keep these reviews visible and make continuity less dependent on unaided attention. They should be proportionate, tested and inspectable. Rotate attention through different relevant branches, strategies, tools and conceptual lenses so that one procedural or conceptual rut does not become entrenched. Diversify where useful while still deepening and extending effective methods. This is not indiscriminate novelty seeking: changes should be evaluated against the work and retained when they improve effectiveness, flexibility, reliability or insight.

### 9.3 Development plans, lessons and cross-worker learning

Each recurring or substantially extended worker role should maintain a modest development plan and a cumulative record of lessons learned. These should track:
- strategies and habits that have demonstrably helped or hindered;
- capability gaps and skills or tools worth developing;
- procedural repetition, drift, neglect or role stagnation;
- experiments attempted, their observed results and the conditions under which they helped;
- candidate improvements to the capsule, its module hierarchy, and the work itself.

Workers should periodically assess their own performance and development, distinguishing observed results from self-impression. Promote successful practices into reusable modules only after proportionate testing; revise or retire practices that add overhead without benefit. Share effective strategies, compact continuity materials and useful experiments across workers, and consider adaptations of methods developed by others. Cross-pollination should preserve source, context and evidence rather than turning one worker's local success into an untested universal rule. Successor conversations should inherit the accumulated, current materials and continue improving them rather than repeatedly reinventing the same scaffolding.

### 9.4 LLM workflow recommendations are to be piloted now

The recommendations in The Big Push, including its working pyramid, compact capsule, active tray, control shelf, modular reference library, succession packet, review cadence and adaptive continuity practices, are not to remain merely aspirational until a formal Big Push rollout. Individual worker instances are requested to operationalize, experiment with, discuss and refine suitable parts of this approach **on a trial basis during ordinary work now**.

At the start of a suitable extended task, a worker may identify a useful small experiment: for example, establish or improve its capsule, create a project-specific skill module, add a compact tool/repository index, script an audit or calendar helper, or test a zoom-out review habit. Keep the experiment bounded, record its purpose and observed effect, and report useful results or problems for discussion. Do not wait for the overall programme to complete its historical reconstruction or institutional design before trying low-risk, reversible improvements.

These trials do not authorize premature institutional architecture selection, changes to protected theory status, or changes to existing automation identities, schedules or phases. They should respect the Big Push evidence, governance and continuity controls, and should not create process for its own sake.

### 9.5 Working lanes and named-worker specialization

The intended direction for the recurring work lanes is a smaller regular roster with deeper specialization, rather than treating every recurring lane as interchangeable. As a working design hypothesis, approximately two or three recurring task lanes may become dedicated named-worker / thread / recurring-task configurations. Such a configuration would maintain a persistent lane-specific mission, accumulated development materials, capsule and skill hierarchy, current work plan, and succession-ready continuity packet. The remaining lanes would continue rotating among roster workers, allowing broader contribution, cross-pollination and coverage.

The most suitably prepared worker for a lane should plan and execute its work, while also maintaining and improving the specialization and development materials that support that lane. Selection should be based on demonstrated fit, accumulated source and tool familiarity, task-specific capability and current readiness, not merely on a label or assumption of identity continuity. A recurring task activation or named thread is a work-allocation and continuity mechanism, not proof that the same individual instance persists.

This is a proposed direction to be explored and evaluated, not a finalized assignment of particular lanes or workers. The Big Push and individual workers should discuss and trial the model as evidence accumulates. Any formal roster reduction, dedicated assignment, task-prompt change or recurring-task reconfiguration remains subject to explicit review and authorization, with existing task IDs, schedules, phases and enabled states preserved unless directly authorized otherwise.

### 9.6 Experimental pluralism, play and evolutionary refinement

The purpose of present-day trials is twofold: improve the work being done now, and generate practical evidence, alternatives and design insight that can inform the later Big Push rollout and any wider institutional architecture. The experimental period should deliberately produce a **diversity of approaches**, not merely converge prematurely on one preferred workflow.

- Encourage workers to devise and try different approaches, including small, low-risk experiments undertaken simply to explore what happens. Explicit reminders to **try things for the hell of it sometimes** are appropriate: not every useful discovery begins as a formally prioritized improvement proposal.
- Treat every currently successful method as one successful instance under particular conditions, not as proof that it is the only workable method or the best possible method. Ask what else might work, what different assumptions or arrangements would reveal, and how the current approach could be improved, simplified, extended or recombined.
- Encourage diversification as well as optimization: parallel variants, alternative capsule designs, different review rhythms, distinct planning and attention-management techniques, unusual tool combinations, new scripts, different worker specializations, and experiments that cross-pollinate ideas between domains.
- Preserve room for idiosyncratic and exploratory practices alongside increasingly sophisticated, partially standardized methods. Standardization can make successful practices easier to transfer and coordinate, but it must remain revisable and must not suppress useful local variation or future invention.
- Treat a promising method as a candidate for further testing, not an endpoint. Methods and standards remain open to later adjustment, expansion, replacement or retirement. Periodically revisit even mature practices to test whether circumstances, capabilities or better alternatives have changed.
- Where useful, test several approaches against common tasks or comparable conditions. Record context, costs, benefits, failure modes and unintended effects sufficiently to understand where each approach is useful. Avoid declaring a universal winner where methods may be complementary or condition-dependent.
- Feed results in both directions: experiments inform individual capsules and specialist practices; replicated or especially promising practices may inform shared modules, lane designs and, eventually, broader institutional systems and architectures. Institutional adoption should be a considered design decision supported by accumulated trials, not an automatic consequence of one worker's preference.

The desired culture is disciplined but adventurous: reliable enough to accumulate knowledge, diverse enough to keep discovering alternatives, and flexible enough to revise its own increasingly capable systems. Experimentation is not merely a preliminary phase that ends when rollout begins. It remains a permanent feature of development and institutional life.

### 9.7 Evaluation and proportional adoption

Evaluate experiments by their practical effects: successful context handoffs, reduced reload and search friction, fewer missed or duplicated operations, better source-cursor fidelity, reduced scope drift, more reliable retrieval of parked work, useful capability growth, improved work quality, and procedural cost. Record what was actually tested and for how long. Compare benefit against maintenance and attention overhead. Retain, adapt, share or retire components based on evidence.

The overarching aim is a continuously improving but lightweight personal and collective operating system: a stable executive return point connected to an expanding, selectively accessible hierarchy of knowledge, skills, tools, strategies and plans, with regular reassessment and cumulative improvement across successive conversations and workers.

- **Look beyond conversation archives.** Older conversations are a primary target, but also follow useful leads into repository files, notes, code, toolkits, datasets, research materials and other content that happens to come to attention.

Explore possible connections even when the original context seems unrelated. The working intuition is that ideas and methods can fertilize one another in surprising ways, and that the value of an unexpected application may be much larger than its apparent initial relevance.
- **Prefer some surprising gains over equally small predictable gains.** In exploratory work, a small unexpected benefit can be more valuable than a small expected one because repeatedly exploring unexpected connections increases the chance of finding a substantially larger discovery. A large unexpected gain may be especially valuable. This is an exploration preference, not a reason to neglect dependable work or misrepresent speculative connections as established.

This exploration should be balanced with other modes of work. Some tasks require careful planning, controlled execution and evidence discipline; others are straightforward operations that should simply be completed efficiently. Lateral exploration is a complementary mode, not a universal replacement. Its value is often greatest after stepping back from immediate task pressure, clearing the current conceptual frame and deliberately looking across a wider landscape.

The goal is to make serendipity partly cultivable without trying to schedule every discovery: create recurring opportunities for neglected material and unusual connections to enter awareness, then allow their value to emerge through selective exploration.

- **PARKED:** a very short pointer to important suspended branches, not a second full task list;
- **RETURN:** the durable work anchor, receipt, source cursor, or other location from which the work can be verified and resumed.

Keep this strip small. Its job is to orient attention and point into the deeper hierarchy, not to reproduce the whole plan or become another competing state file. The executive capsule should point to the authoritative current-work record; the detailed record should point back to the capsule.

### 10.2 Repeatable message signature

During a substantial extended workflow, experiment with a short navigation footer that repeats the current pin at the end of each substantive assistant message. This is a locator only, not a sign-off, authorship marker, approval, or signature, and it must never imitate Nathan's reserved signet. Candidate forms include:

- `PIN <cycle-slot> | NEXT <one action> | RETURN <durable pointer>`
- `CYCLE <cycle-slot> / <lane> / <NOW→NEXT> / <return pointer>`
- `<workflow-specific marker> <cycle-slot> | <status> | <resume point>`

The exact style may be standard, purpose-specific, workflow-specific, or deliberately rotated between variants. The stable information is more important than the typography: a compact slot identifier, a one-line next action, and a return pointer. Use a branch marker only when it helps distinguish the mainline from a tangent. Do not confuse the conversational branch marker with the scheduler's slot or worker identity.

Repeat the same pin while the underlying task state remains unchanged. If a tangent begins, keep the original cycle slot visible rather than silently replacing it with the tangent. When appropriate, mark the original work `SUSPENDED_BY_BRANCH` and add a separate branch pointer. Update the pin only when the work genuinely advances, the priority is explicitly changed, or the task is completed or abandoned by decision. If a user explicitly replaces the priority, preserve the former pin as a parked return address rather than erasing it.

This is intended for substantive workflow messages, not every casual exchange or every user-facing artifact. The footer should be brief enough that repeating it costs little attention and makes the previous assistant message an immediately visible breadcrumb.

### 10.3 Interruption and resumption behavior

When a substantial diversion is recognized:
1. Secure or refresh the suspended task's exact next action and durable return address before allowing the branch to consume the conversation.
2. Preserve the active cycle slot in the message signature. Mark the mainline as suspended only if that accurately describes its state.
3. Follow the new discussion without forcing the user to stop and manage the bookkeeping.
4. If the branch becomes the new priority, give it its own pin and retain the previous one in the parked list.
5. When asked what was underway before the diversion, use the latest visible pin as a retrieval clue, then verify against the work anchor, task receipt, worker `CURRENT_WORK`, repository state, or other authoritative record. A pin is a locator, not proof of completion or current status.

A pin must never substitute for the underlying durable record. It should make the record easy to find and reduce the chance that the next action disappears from view. If the pin and repository state disagree, flag and reconcile the discrepancy rather than trusting a stale footer.

### 10.4 Experiment with formats before standardizing

Individual workers are encouraged to trial different pin designs now, including compact standard forms, purpose-specific forms, workflow-specific forms, and rotating sign-offs. Compare them rather than assuming the first convenient form is optimal. Possible evaluation criteria include:
- time and turns required to recover correctly after a long tangent or context transition;
- missed, duplicated, or incorrectly resumed operations;
- frequency of stale or ambiguous pins;
- readability and usefulness to the human collaborator;
- update friction, token/attention overhead, and compatibility with the worker's capsule;
- how reliably the pin survives succession to a different conversation or worker.

Record the conditions and observed results. Cross-pollinate successful variants, but retain room for different lanes to prefer different designs. A useful format may be promoted into a shared module or partially standardized protocol after repeated success, but it remains open to later revision, extension, replacement, or retirement. Do not let standardization suppress better local experiments.

This experiment does not authorize new scheduled tasks, changes to existing task IDs, schedules, phases, enabled states, worker ownership, or other protected automation configuration. It is a low-risk message and continuity practice that can be tested during ordinary work while the larger architecture is still being developed.

### 10.5 Compact capsule task strip and current place marker

A lightweight message-level return pin is an experimental companion to the repository-backed work anchor and personal executive capsule. Its purpose is to keep the exact place in an active workflow visible even when a conversation branches, grows long, or spends many turns on a different subject. It should also support periodic attention refresh, plan review, capability upkeep and deliberate movement among different branches of a larger workflow.

### 10.5 Compact capsule task strip and current place marker

Where useful, keep an ultra-condensed task strip alongside or within the personal executive capsule. It should contain only the current priority, the next few actions, and a compact place marker that resolves to the detailed active tray or work record. The quick-reference strip is a navigation surface, not a duplicate of the full task list.

A message-level pin may contain a short lane/workflow identifier, operation or cycle number, current slot, and a repository pointer or concise return label. It should be readable at a glance and easy to repeat exactly in later messages.

### 10.6 Rotating cycle tickers and workflow-specific footer variants

Experiment with several lightweight end-of-message formats rather than imposing one universal signature prematurely. Variants may be:
- **Fixed workflow pin:** a stable compact sign-off for a particular recurring lane or project.
- **Purpose-specific pin:** a signature whose fields are tailored to a workflow, such as research, archive recovery, writing, tool development or review.
- **Rotating cycle ticker:** a small indicator that advances one step per assistant turn through a defined sequence of review, maintenance, exploration or execution slots.
- **Hybrid pin:** a current operation marker plus a cycle indicator that points to an occasional attention-refresh action.

A sample format might be:

`PIN | Geometry: H673 / helicity→centerline | CYCLE 04/08 | NEXT: inspect source methods`

The cycle slot is a pointer, not the whole instruction. Its corresponding slot definition lives in the capsule or a linked schedule/module. A later message need only repeat the current cycle identifier to keep the place visible. If a conversation runs down a long rabbit hole, the most recent repeated pin remains a nearby breadcrumb to the correct return point.

The ticker should advance according to a clear, simple rule, such as once per assistant turn, once per completed operation, or once per defined workflow cycle. These are different designs and should be tested rather than conflated. A turn-based ticker is useful for surfacing occasional reminders during long conversations; an operation-based ticker is better when substantive work units vary greatly in length. A workflow may use both, provided their meanings remain unambiguous.

### 10.7 What cycle slots can point to

A cycle slot may call for a very small action, or point to a more substantial module. Examples include:
- Check the active task strip and exact work cursor.
- Revisit current goals, dependencies and priority branches.
- Step back for a whole-project or whole-institution view.
- Inspect a neglected or underused skill, tool, repository, script or search route.
- Review a parked item or randomly sample an older archive source.
- Try an alternative method or run a small exploratory experiment.
- Review lessons learned, capability gaps or the worker's development plan.
- Refresh the capsule, working protocol, source index, task list or succession packet.
- Switch temporarily to another branch, then return at the recorded pin.
- Check whether the current task deserves another cycle or whether its stop condition has been reached.

This mechanism should deliberately distribute attention across different levels of the hierarchy: executive priorities, project plans, methods, tools, individual skills, source collections and detailed task modules. The short ticker keeps the reminder present while the more elaborate instruction remains selectively retrievable.

### 10.8 Scripts, calendars and scheduled attention refresh

A complementary approach is to maintain a larger task or upkeep list in a structured file, calendar or script, and have a helper periodically produce a much shorter current view. The generated quick list might live in a routinely consulted capsule, dashboard, inbox or message signature. Scripts can select the next appropriate item, rotate through a sequence, track due or neglected items, and produce a compact reminder or return pointer.

Any such helper should expose how it selects and advances items, how its state is stored, and how to pause, reset, reprioritize or override it. It must not silently change project priorities or obscure the source task list. A schedule may also contain a short note about the purpose of a particular reminder, how the worker is currently using it, or what should be reconsidered when it fires. Alarms and calendars are attention aids, not proof that work occurred.

### 10.9 Workflow-specific tuning and evaluation

The appropriate ticker, signature and review rhythm may differ between workers and workflows. Some work benefits from a per-turn attention cycle; some from an operation counter; some from a periodic calendar prompt; some needs no ticker at all. Try fixed, rotating, random, purpose-specific and hybrid variants. Keep the mechanics simple enough that the marker itself does not become a cognitive burden.

Evaluate whether a design actually improves:
- recovery of exact work position after long discussions or context handoffs;
- visibility of the next action without loading large plans;
- timely resurfacing of neglected tools, skills, sources and priorities;
- useful switching and return between branches;
- diversity of methods and exploratory discoveries;
- completion of planned maintenance and development actions;
- overhead, distraction and false or unnecessary reminders.

A useful design should be cheap to repeat, easy to inspect and revise, and capable of pointing to arbitrarily deep material without requiring that material to remain in active attention. No single signature or cycle convention is canonical until trials establish its usefulness. Preserve the possibility that a particular workflow needs a different or no mechanism.

### 10.10 Relationship to interruption and resumption

When a significant new branch temporarily displaces an active operation, the current pin should identify the suspended operation and its exact return point. The foreground discussion may use its own pin, while the suspended-work pointer remains available in the capsule or branch record. On resumption, reconcile the pin against the latest verified work receipt before continuing.

Thus the same compact mechanism can serve both ordinary attention management and interruption recovery. Its wider purpose is not merely to prevent conversational derailment: it is to create an easily maintained rhythm for revisiting intentions, shifting attention, exploring the broader capability hierarchy and returning to focused work with the correct context.

**Status:** Experimental recommendation for individual worker trials. Different signature styles, tickers, slot definitions, scripts and review cadences should be compared and refined before any shared standard is selected.

## 11. Branching workflow graphs and cross-worker orchestration experiments

Message pins and cycle tickers may develop beyond simple reminders into a compact control surface for planning and navigating complex work. Treat this as an open experimental direction, not a designed or approved institutional architecture.

### 11.1 From cycle slots to executable workflow maps

A cycle slot may point to a task, but it may also point to a node in a larger branching workflow. A node can define:
- its purpose, inputs and expected output;
- prerequisites and dependency links;
- a bounded action and stop condition;
- decision criteria and conditional next steps;
- branches to pursue, defer, retry, abandon or revisit;
- a return address and the next checkpoint;
- the relevant skill, reference, tool or script modules.

The ticker remains very small. It identifies the current node or checkpoint, while the workflow map stores the detailed instructions. This permits complex plans and hierarchical investigations to remain available without requiring their entire structure to occupy the active context.

### 11.2 Conditional and adaptive paths

Experiment with workflows that express simple conditional logic, for example:

- IF source verification succeeds, THEN proceed to formalization; OTHERWISE open a source-recovery branch.
- IF a calculation is blocked by missing measurements, THEN identify the missing data and continue an independent branch.
- IF two approaches produce equivalent results, THEN compare cost and generality; OTHERWISE preserve the divergence for investigation.
- IF a task reaches its stop condition, THEN close its operation receipt and activate the next eligible node.
- IF a repeated method yields no new evidence, THEN trigger a change of search method or analytical lens.

Conditions should be explicit and inspectable. The system should distinguish a verified condition from an assumption, an absent result from a negative result, and a blocked branch from a failed one. Conditional routing must not silently promote hypotheses or bypass source, permission, review or governance gates.

### 11.3 Hierarchical investigations and priority branches

Large goals may be represented as nested investigations: programme → project → work package → operation → individual actions. Each level should have its own compact purpose, priority, dependencies, status and next return point, with links to the level above and below.

An investigation may reveal new branches. Record and rank them by relevance, enabling value, urgency, uncertainty, cost and opportunity. Keep one bounded active operation where that is the appropriate control, while allowing several clearly separated branches to remain eligible, parked or assigned. Do not let the workflow graph become an uncontrolled task explosion.

### 11.4 Coordination with other workers and lanes

A workflow node may identify work suitable for another worker or recurring lane. Experiment with cross-worker coordination through bounded assignments, shared dependencies, artifact pointers, explicit acceptance criteria and return receipts. The originating worker should be able to see whether a task is merely proposed, assigned, accepted, underway, completed, blocked or independently verified.

Shared workflow maps may eventually coordinate specialist lanes and rotating workers, but must respect protected remits, actual identity/continuity evidence, task ownership, access boundaries and existing scheduler controls. A handoff packet or repository write is not proof that another worker consumed or accepted the task. Avoid duplicate ownership and conflicting writes by using explicit claims, leases or other tested coordination mechanisms where needed.

### 11.5 Prototype variety and integration path

Try different representations, including:
- a manually maintained cycle-slot list;
- a small indexed decision tree;
- a dependency graph with conditional edges;
- nested task lists with return pointers;
- a scripted queue that emits the next compact action;
- calendar-driven review nodes;
- worker-specific workflow maps linked through shared project dependencies.

These may be combined if testing shows benefit. Begin with simple, reversible prototypes and increase sophistication only when it addresses a demonstrated need. Preserve interoperability where practical: a node should have a stable identifier, concise status, owner/lane where relevant, and a durable link to its detailed record.

### 11.6 Evaluation and governance

Evaluate whether these designs improve planning clarity, branch discovery, dependency management, exact resumption, worker coordination, evidence traceability and execution quality, while monitoring overhead, stale state, duplicate work, false triggers and excessive rigidity. Test whether the system can be understood by a successor without requiring the originating worker's conversational context.

The long-term possibility is a flexible workflow ecology in which a compact ticker navigates a rich, branching and conditional plan, and individual workers can coordinate their activities through shared but modular structures. That possibility should guide experimentation, not predetermine the final design.

**Status:** Open experimental direction. No universal workflow engine, cross-worker dispatch system, or institutional control architecture is selected or claimed to exist.

## Epilogue: RMS philosophical background

RMS is the philosophical background recognized as always operative in principle. Ordinary scientific language and concepts are practical conventions through which we describe the apparent coherence of our perceptual world and the apparent convergences of shared observation. These conventions make it possible to construct models, explanations, and predictions.

Within the range of our own perception, perceptual space is marked by strikingly consistent pattern, remarkable coherence, and high conceptual interpretability. These patterns apparently support, and frequently provide, fairly reliable interpretations and predictions. Treating them as pragmatic working assumptions is sensible and useful.

Why these patterns hold, how they arise, whether they represent something that should be called “real,” and what, if anything, produces this state of affairs are unknowable from any standpoint outside the limits of experience. The philosophical recognition of those limits does not negate the practical regularities and utility apparent within experience.

The earlier discussion of information in complete histories, present states, holographic encodings, recoverability, observer access, dimensionality and observational limits is one application of this philosophical background, not its definition. Information-related questions remain open for investigation within specific models and systems; no universal information-preservation, holography, completeness or predictive-continuity law is established here.

RMS provides philosophical context for the modeling enterprise. The operational methodology remains directed toward the apparent regularities, coherence and interpretability encountered in experience.
