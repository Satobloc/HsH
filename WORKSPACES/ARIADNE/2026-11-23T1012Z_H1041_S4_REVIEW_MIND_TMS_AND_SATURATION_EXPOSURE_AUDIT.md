## 3. New research evidence and its limits

### 3.1 Human-AI transactive memory

The CMU AI-SDM workshop program describes a 159-team controlled study, “AI and Transactive Memory Systems,” using a 2x2 design crossing AI access with a TMS intervention. The program reports that AI access improved accuracy while AI was available but that accuracy after withdrawal was lower than in no-AI teams; TMS reportedly preserved unique information contributions and supported performance. This is a workshop-program description of a working paper, not a full peer-reviewed article or an independently inspected raw dataset. Treat it as a promising discovery lead, not a settled general result.

Source: https://www.cmu.edu/ai-sdm/events/2026-annual-workshop.html

### 3.2 TMS accuracy is not TMS consensus

Lewis's validated 15-item TMS scale distinguishes specialization, credibility, and coordination. It is a useful team self-report instrument, but it does not by itself establish that a team's shared “who knows what” map is objectively correct.

A 2018 study of 26 interdisciplinary mental-health teams reported that TMS accuracy was positively associated with team performance, while TMS consensus was not statistically significant. This is adjacent team research, not a human-LLM experiment and not a causal estimate.

Sources:
- Lewis (2003), “Measuring transactive memory systems in the field: Scale development and validation”: https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.88.4.587
- 2018 TMS accuracy/consensus study: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jclp.22656

### 3.3 Transcript-derived TMS measurement

A 2023 text-based TMS measure was developed and evaluated across 262 groups and 1,091 individuals, with human-coded and dictionary-based measures compared against the Lewis scale. Its validation domain includes programming tasks. It is a candidate process measure, not a drop-in validated instrument for policy deliberation. Any adaptation requires domain-specific coding, blinded raters, inter-rater reliability, and validation against the target task.

Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10446314/

### 3.4 Hybrid-team conceptual evidence

Hopf and colleagues' qualitative case study of a human-intelligent-agent team in a digital health platform develops the idea of a transactive intelligent memory system. It is useful for distinguishing shared external memory from a team-level “who knows what” structure, but it is a single-case qualitative study, not a randomized causal test.

Source: https://jit.ndhu.edu.tw/article/view/3264

### 3.5 Interference design

Hudgens and Halloran's partial-interference framework and the 2026 randomized-saturation preprint by Lu, Shi, and Ding make the between-group assumption explicit. A design that randomizes AIR saturation within groups must still address cross-group sharing or network exposure if participants can carry information between groups.

Sources:
- Hudgens & Halloran (2008): https://doi.org/10.1198/016214508000000292
- Lu, Shi & Ding (2026), arXiv:2603.19573: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19573

## 4. Three-person AIR saturation formalization

Let each three-person group have an assignment vector z in {0,1}^3, where z_i=1 means participant i receives individual AIR before the group discussion. Define:

- A_i = z_i, the focal participant's own AIR status.
- K_i = sum of z_j over j not equal to i, the number of AIR-treated peers.
- S_i = the set of AIR-treated peers.

If each saturation level s in {0,1,2,3} has positive probability and, conditional on s, every subset of exactly s participants has positive probability, all eight assignment vectors are supported. Therefore every one of the six individual exposure cells (A_i,K_i) is reachable for every focal participant.

The individual-level estimands can then be distinguished:

- Direct effect at peer exposure k: DE(k) = E[Y_i(1,k) - Y_i(0,k)].
- Spillover among untreated participants: IE_0(k) = E[Y_i(0,k+1) - Y_i(0,k)].
- Spillover among treated participants: IE_1(k) = E[Y_i(1,k+1) - Y_i(1,k)].
- Group policy effect: compare E[G_g(s)] across randomized group saturation levels.

These are different estimands. Group-level outcomes must not be treated as three independent participant outcomes.

### 4.1 Exact exposure probabilities under two possible allocations

For equal allocation across the four saturation levels, p_s=1/4:

| Own AIR A | Treated peers K | Exposure probability |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 1/4 |
| 0 | 1 | 1/6 |
| 0 | 2 | 1/12 |
| 1 | 0 | 1/12 |
| 1 | 1 | 1/6 |
| 1 | 2 | 1/4 |

This balances group-level saturation arms but makes the extreme individual exposure cells relatively rare.

If each of the eight assignment vectors is equally likely, equivalent to saturation probabilities 1:3:3:1, the exposure probabilities are:

| Own AIR A | Treated peers K | Exposure probability |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 1/8 |
| 0 | 1 | 1/4 |
| 0 | 2 | 1/8 |
| 1 | 0 | 1/8 |
| 1 | 1 | 1/4 |
| 1 | 2 | 1/8 |

This balances the two cells within each direct-effect contrast, but gives fewer all-control and all-AIR groups. Allocation should follow the pre-registered primary estimands and pilot-based power analysis, not convenience. No power estimate is warranted without outcome variance, group ICC, attrition, and target effect sizes.

### 4.2 Count-only exposure loses identity

The peer-count map is the projection pi_i(S_i)=|S_i|. It is many-to-one.

For focal participant 0, consider two assignments:

- participant 1 receives AIR; participant 2 does not;
- participant 2 receives AIR; participant 1 does not.

In both assignments, participant 0 is untreated and has exactly one AIR-treated peer. Yet an outcome that depends on whether participant 1 was treated differs between the assignments.

Thus (A_i,K_i) is not generally a sufficient exposure map. A count-only potential outcome Y_i(a,k) requires an explicit anonymity/exchangeability assumption about peer identities and peer content. Otherwise retain the full peer set and relevant content lineage.

The source-resolved exposure record should distinguish:
1. which participants received AIR;
2. which AI-generated claims or suggestions each received;
3. what was visible to each participant;
4. which claims were repeated or paraphrased in group discussion;
5. which claims were taken up and incorporated into the decision.

The common model snapshot and prompt are shared upstream causes, not independent sources of evidence. Two participants repeating the same AI-originated claim do not create two independent confirmations.

## 6. Review-MIND design refinement

The proposed AIR intervention remains individual pre-deliberative reflection followed by human-only group discussion. The AI is not present as a group facilitator or group member during discussion.

Recommended additions:

1. Measure baseline and post-discussion TMS accuracy and TMS consensus separately.
2. Use the Lewis 15-item scale for specialization, credibility, and coordination as a self-report measure.
3. Add an objective expertise-directory task: each participant identifies which teammate knows what, scored against independent pre-task knowledge tests. Report map accuracy separately from inter-member agreement.
4. Measure unique-information disclosure and correct integration, not merely the number of distinct utterances.
5. Record AIR output provenance, participant visibility, idea overlap, uptake, and decision incorporation.
6. Keep group decision accuracy, discourse quality, confidence calibration, and post-withdrawal transfer as separate outcomes.
7. Use group-clustered randomization inference or estimators that respect the known assignment probabilities. Do not treat three members of one group as independent groups.
8. Isolate groups or record cross-group contacts. If between-group spillovers are plausible, add a network exposure mapping rather than silently assuming partial interference.
9. Treat text-derived TMS coding as exploratory until adapted and validated for the deliberation domain. Do not use the individual TMS-GenAI scale as a team-level outcome.

### Disconfirming outcomes

The proposed convergence/withdrawal mechanism would be weakened or rejected if:

- higher AIR saturation does not increase overlap in pre-discussion content;
- unique information is disclosed and correctly integrated at the same or higher rate;
- post-withdrawal decision accuracy is not lower than the no-AIR condition;
- TMS accuracy does not moderate uptake or decision performance;
- apparent effects disappear after accounting for baseline expertise, group composition, task difficulty, or differential attrition.

A later randomized TMS intervention could test whether an expertise-map training changes these outcomes, but it should not be added to the first saturation study without a separate power and protocol justification.
