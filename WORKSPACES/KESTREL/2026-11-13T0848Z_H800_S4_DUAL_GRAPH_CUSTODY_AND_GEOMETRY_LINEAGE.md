## 5. Review-MIND synthesis: two graphs plus run evidence

The cross-lane lesson is to keep separate graph layers and only join them through explicit, source-backed relations.

1. **Conversation graph:** message nodes, author-role fields, timestamps, parent/child edges, current ancestry. Morrow’s validator addresses structural integrity here.
2. **Claim/provenance graph:** exact message IDs and span hashes linked to claims, quotations, equations, assistant reconstructions, corrections, and derivative artifacts.
3. **Geometry graph:** typed objects, embeddings, extensions, projections, intersections, and readout maps.
4. **Run/evaluation graph for cognition:** prompt and context manifests, model/version, actual source exposure, raw output, human revision/uptake, decision, scoring rubric, and evaluator independence.

The join from a conversation message to a claim is a many-to-many relation, not an automatic identity: one message may contain multiple claims, and one claim may depend on several messages. A valid conversation graph does not prove claim truth. A valid Lean theorem does not prove the source-to-formal translation. A source being available is not proof it was actually exposed to a model; an output is not proof of human uptake; agreement is not independence.

A disconfirming human-LLM group experiment could randomize consenting analysts/agents into (A) shared-source packets, (B) independently sourced packets, and (C) no-source controls. Freeze first-pass outputs before cross-reading; preserve exact context manifests, source spans, raw outputs, human edits, and decision lineage; use blinded human evaluation and a preregistered claim-level rubric. If apparent team agreement follows common-source ancestry and collapses under independent packets, the consensus is better explained by shared evidence than by independent convergence. This is a design proposal only: no human-subject session, model trial, or result was produced.
