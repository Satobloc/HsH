The sounds of glossolalia do not necessarily reflect the inventory of the speaker's language, for they frequently include phones not found in a speaker's native tongue. English speakers, for instance, often use /a/ as a high central, unrounded vowel, the so-called continental sound, which does not occur in English, and Spanish speakers in Mexico may use /ö/ (the long, closed o, as in the German word Öse ), which is not a Spanish vowel. In addition, shrieks as well as barking, whistling, grunting, growling, and many other so-called animal sounds have also been reported.

The production of random phonemes without any semantico-syntactic organization requires an inhibitory control of the prepotent tendency for producing real-language phonemics and syntactic configurations. According to the specificity hypothesis, the repeated practice of glossolalia may thus have induced plastic modifications within the left fronto-temporo-parietal network controlling language production. This hypothesis more precisely predicts structural modifications within the anterior cingular executive areas controlling language production and within the dorsal phonological production stream, notably including the left inferior frontal gyrus, temporo-parietal junction and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (Abutalebi, 2008). The arcuate fasciculus may also be involved since it connects the caudal part of the superior temporal gyrus to the frontal lobe (Nieuwenhuys et al., 1988), which supports bidirectional functional interactions between left inferior frontal spoken word production and temporo-parietal phonemic retrieval and sequencing areas (Matsumoto et al., 2004). Finally, beyond the control networks also typically involved in bilinguals, glossolalia may require militasking and inhibiting usual language production. Corresponding mechanisms are for instance at play in simultaneous interpreters and are thought to depend on the frontal poles in addition to ventrolateral prefrontal areas (Becker et al., 2016).

An association between trance and glossolalia is now accepted by many researchers as a correct assumption (see, for instance, Williams, 1981). Thus, if one recognizes trance as the primary, generating process of the features of glossolalia, one could then conclude that a vocalization consisting only of nonsense syllables, no matter how varied, may in fact represent a cultural convention and that other types of speech could also be uttered while the practitioner is in a trance, with the trance, of course, still expressing itself in some form or other. The Colombian superstar traces all of the messy phases of heartbreak on her seventh album

Karol G
Karol G for 'No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto’

It’s been a little over a year since Karol G invited us into the glitzy, ambitious world of Tropicoqueta where she took on the role of a showgirl to thread together the varied sounds of Latin pop and enticed the world to dance along. But what happens when the showgirl goes home, wipes off her makeup, and is left to sit with her dark blue feelings? Welcome to No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto, which translates to I Don’t Regret Feeling So Much. 

On her seventh studio album, the Colombian superstar steps away from the globe-trotting adventure of Tropicoqueta and peels back the curtain to reveal a more intimate space: the moody one inside her bedroom. Here, the singer gets more vulnerable than ever as she details the past year, and all of its challenges, chief among them a highly public break-up from fellow Colombian musician Feid, plus career criticism, family issues, and more.

Written partly during a solo trip to Hawaii, No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto is a sharply focused album that’s as transparent as its title suggests, never shying away from the tumultuous emotions that follow the end of a relationship. We’ve seen Karol here before on Mañana Será Bonito, the historic, life-altering LP that brought her stadium-level success. But where that album centered on coming out of a break-up unscathed, No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto is about choosing to wallow in the sadness — and uncovering a whole new power in that choice.

True to the messy nature of a break-up, the 14-song track list mirrors all of the tug and pull phases of heartbreak. There’s that first burst of rage on the ferocious hyperpop album opener “Te Llevas To,” where Karol declares, “Eres un cabrón … te importa zero.” A few tracks later, she’s written love off forever on the alt-perreo “Bebiendo Lágrimas.” By the time Drake hops on the sultry late-summer stunner “Ahí,” the Colombian singer just wants to forget about the past and have a good time. With Champagne Papi doing his best Spanish-language vocals, she does just that. It doesn’t last long.

Francisca Valenzuela’s ‘Maldita’ Is an Unflinching — and Cathartic — Look at Motherhood 
The Chilean-American singer-songwriter goes deep and finds healing on her new album

By Ernesto Lechner

July 31, 2026
Francisca Valenzuela
Francisco Finat*

A few months after experiencing motherhood for the first time, Chilean singer-songwriter Francisca Valenzuela began planning a new album. Over Zoom, suffering from exhaustion and postpartum depression, she spoke to a trusted collaborator about making an exuberant party record that would channel the bubbly physicality of Italian icon Raffaella Carrá. “Fran, are you sure you wanna do this?” the producer replied.

The 39-year-old artist changed gears and decided to focus instead on her ongoing emotional breakdown. One of life’s most cruel impositions is that new mothers are expected to sport a beatific smile of perpetual bliss while the hormones are wreaking havoc with body and mind. “I curse the day in which I was born/I curse the day in which I gave birth/I curse my own youth/My eternal ineptitude,” Valenzuela bites back on “Maldita” (Cursed), the album’s title track. The real surprise here is not the unflinching nature of the lyrics, but how gorgeous her vocals and piano sound now that she allows herself the catharsis of letting go.

Anyone familiar with Valenzuela’s previous art-pop gems Vida Tan Bonita (2022) and Adentro (2024) will tell you that she’s an obsessive perfectionist (once, after messing up a keyboard line during a show, she insisted on playing the entire song from the beginning so that she could get it right.) The delicious paradox of Maldita is that while the lyrics and spoken word passages trace the spiraling fracture of a self-professed “hysterical witch,” the music itself shimmers with regal precision and grace.

On “Malacara,” the orchestral backdrop acts like the chorus of a Greek tragedy, echoing her doubts through dissonance and ominous melodic turns. The arrangement of lead single “Buganvilia” is as saturated as the narrator who feels her mind slipping away while the bougainvillea flowers outside. On the sweeping “No Te Aburras De Mí,” she prays that her partner won’t get tired of their life together.

By the end of Maldita, she has weathered the storm and embraced a new life. “Nací Necesitándote” is the kind of heart-melting ode to her baby daughter that could have only germinated after dealing with the pain inside. On “Komorebi,” a Japanese word that acknowledges the fleeting beauty of nature, her contentment feels earned. Far from cursed, Valenzuela is one of the most generous artists of her generation — and a Latin American treasure.

Phoebe Bridgers Makes Up for Lost Time on Her Long-Awaited New Album
After a six-year break between solo projects, the songwriter returns with Lost Weekend, a spectacular song cycle on love and loss

By Angie Martoccio

Illustration by NICOLE RIFKIN
August 5, 2026
Phoebe Bridgers Review

L
et’s go missing,” Phoebe Bridgers declares on Lost Weekend. That’s exactly what she did, more or less, immediately after ascending to a new level of stardom in 2023 with boygenius, her indie-rock supergroup, alongside Lucy Dacus and Julien Baker. At the 2024 Grammys, where Bridgers won more awards than any other artist, she described her upcoming plans on the red carpet: “Now, we walk into the sunset,” she said. 

She’s back now with her first solo album in six years, and there’s a lot of catching up to do. Bridgers makes up for the last several years (you might call it lost time) by giving us her longest and most daring record yet, a tour de force in the art of disappearing, falling in love, and feeling grief so deeply that waking up every day kind of sucks. But as she proves across 16 tracks, it’s not all that bleak — and she makes sure to sprinkle her charm and sharp-witted humor across the record. “I was the belle of the governor’s ball,” she sings on the whimsical highlight “The Governor’s Waltz,” one of several songs full of cryptic hints about what her life is like these days. “Hiding in the bathroom like Shelley Duvall.”


All work and no play makes Bridgers a dull boy, so she finds time to experiment sonically, like on the opener, “The Outside.” She enters on vocoder — bolstered by synths from her new collaborator, Jack Antonoff — as she delivers lines that possibly nod to her massive year with her boygenius buds, and what she was going through while it happened: “Crying in a suit and tie/But you should see the other guy/One more time for old time’s sake/I’ll watch him get carried away.” 


Bridgers said she’s been listening to a lot of ambient music lately, and you can hear how that shaped Lost Weekend, along with Radiohead (fans were quick to point out the Kid A shirt she wore at one of her recent pop-up shows). These influences are eerily present on glitchy interludes and instrumentals, like on the meditative centerpiece “Panorama,” where she doesn’t sing for the first two minutes, letting the sparkling piano, string arrangement, horns, and synths take the lead. The new touches enhance her favorite lyrical motifs (sleeping, chemicals, hospitals all make an appearance here) and joyous screams, like on standout “As Above,” which she debuted earlier this summer at Madison Square Garden. “You won’t have to believe in magic,” she triumphantly yells. “Leave it to me, and I’ll make it happen.”

When Bridgers released her 2020 breakthrough, Punisher, she said one of its themes was numbness. But on Lost Weekend, she feels it all, encountering every spectrum of emotion. “I have no secrets, only feelings,” she admits on the country-tinged beauty “Haunted” (fans of “Graceland Too,” this one is for you). The loss of her father, who died in 2023 at 60, looms large, examined on heartbreaking tracks like “Still Standing” (“I can’t imagine you dead/But you are every day”) and “Lost Weekend (Reprise)”: “I am my old man’s oldest son/I fought the wall, and the wall won.” She even includes a voicemail from him on “Never Going Back!” that makes the grief even more gutting. 

But as lonely as she might have felt — this is an album with four song titles containing the word “lost” — she wasn’t alone. Happiness flows through the track list, too, particularly on the subject of her largely off-the-radar relationship with comedian Bo Burnham. She falls hard for him on “Bobby,” an uptempo rocker that basks in dizzying euphoria: “Now I don’t know if I can make it tonight/ But what are you doing the rest of my life?” And on gems like “Liberty Tree,” she contends with being a rock star — the same songwriter who once romanticized shower beers and payment plans now has “too much money, and nothing to lose.”

It contains one of her most stunning couplets yet: “Now I can’t see any stars in the sky/When a dream comes true, a fantasy dies,” reminding us why she’s one of the most compelling songwriters in music today.

Shaboozey Thinks Big on ‘The Outlaw Cherie Lee & Other Western Tales’
The country hitmaker takes a major swing with his new Wild West concept album, and mostly connects

By Jon Dolan

July 31, 2026
Shaboozey: A Country Innovator Thinks Very Big
Hunter Moreno*

On his third album, 2024’s Where I’ve Been, Isn’t Where I’m Going, and its monster hit, “A Bar Song (Tipsy),” Shaboozey is a cowboy for our times. The Virginia-raised Nigerian American artist is blessed with a cavernous baritone and a sense of music history where rap and country converse as equally elastic genres and equally deep traditions. It’s no surprise to hear him thinking big with his ambitious new concept album, The Outlaw Cherie Lee & Other Western Tales. According to Shaboozey, the LP tells the story of a sheriff’s daughter who sees her daddy gunned down, takes to the trail seeking vengeance, and ends up falling for one of the killers — a romance tucked inside an outlaw epic, right on par for a guy who does well with genre mashups.

The actual narrative is more implied than rendered. Cameos from Sam Elliott, Teyana Taylor, and Jamie Foxx help establish the mood, setting up songs like the searing small-town-escape fantasy “Burn It Down,” with Leon Bridges, and the spaghetti-Western-tinged gunslinger story song “Lone Survivor.” As outlaw-country singers go, Shaboozey has a nice mix of Waylon Jennings-style gruff and Willie Nelson-type chill, whether he’s lending his ample low-end rumble to laid-back honky-tonk (“If I Was an Outlaw”), tear-in-my-beer acoustic laments (“Kissed by an Angel”), mournful balladry (the moving Kehlani duet, “Bullets and Blades”), or lonely range-rover fatalism (“Coyote’s Point”).


He’s not exactly aiming for another “Tipsy” in these 20 songs, and there are times when hard-bitten dust-caked authenticity piles up a little too heavily. He’s at his best when his music has more stylistic give to it. “High Noon,” with Gunna, lives easily in the space between Nashville and Atlanta, and the stomp-and-clap standouts like “Cowgirl” and “Born to Die” rattle with a down-home, deep-rolling barroom boom that is his sonic safety zone. One thing’s for sure listening to The Outlaw Cherie Lee: Shaboozey isn’t interested in merely going down in history as the guy who had that one big bar song.

There’s No Ariana Grande Like Pissed-Off Ariana Grande. Her New Album Proves It
Rage is all over the pop superstar’s eighth LP, Petal — and it suits her

By Rob Sheffield

July 31, 2026
Ariana Grande
katia temkin*

Ariana Grande is always at her best when she’s in a mood, and on Petal, she’s not holding back at all. “A little feral,” she calls this album, explaining, “I wrote from a place that I don’t usually, which was like an unfiltered rage.” She isn’t kidding. The catchiest pop tune here, “Oh Well,” is the one where she sings, “Good luck on your way to hell,” before dropping the killer punch line, “Don’t worry, ’cause you’ll keep getting breakup songs about you.”

That rage is all over Petal. But it suits her musically, because Ariana is always best — most herself — when she goes off the dome, talking her shit in DGAF mode. Even the album cover is part of her statement, wearing her hair down and loose — Ariana abandoning her ponytail is as iconic as George Michael setting fire to his leather jacket. For some of us, there’s no Ariana like Pissed-Off Ariana.

Her first taste of Petal was “Hate That I Made You Love Me,” which hit Number One, though it actually turns out to be one of the lesser tunes here. It serves as a thematic warning for the mood Ari’s in these days, sneering, “You studied my crown and borrowed my body.” But she aims her tough talk more at the culture than at any ex-friend or ex-lover. “Why you so hate to see women endure?” she demands. “Is it really my fault you all gave me your hearts of your own accord?” 

Ariana rages against the tabloid rumor mill, which has been buzzing about her nonstop lately. But she’s always had a flair for using controversy as fuel for her art. That comes up all over this album, as she snarls lines like “Bitch, I’m practically drowning in my blessings” (“Petal”) or “All that shit-talk keeps me well fed” (“Like I Do”). She thrives on those bad vibes.

For her big comeback in 2024, she dropped Eternal Sunshine, a complex and masterful song cycle. But as soon as she released the album, she seemed to step back from pop stardom, almost as if she scared herself at how deep she went in those songs. Instead of promoting it, or doing interviews, she threw herself into her Hollywood blockbuster era with the Wicked movies. It almost felt like she felt safer in Oz than facing up to the heartbreak of Eternal Sunshine.

Yard Act Loosen Their Collar and Rock Adventurously on ‘You’re Gonna Need A Little Music’
The rising British band’s most musically adventurous album yet delivers rousing highs, even if a few songs struggle to keep pace

By Sophie Miller

July 19, 2026
yard act you're gonna need a little music review
James Winstanley*

The British rock band Yard Act has never had much interest in offering easy answers. The Leeds quartet built a reputation on sardonic observations about modern life, packaging political unease inside wiry post-punk. But on their third album, You’re Gonna Need A Little Music, they sound surprisingly liberated. It doesn’t abandon those wiry instincts so much as loosen its collar. It’s a lively, melodic record that finds the band trading some of their familiar bite for movement, groove, and the occasional open-road soundtrack. 

The album’s biggest victories arrive from two different directions. On “New Beginnings” and the title track, Yard Act’s newfound musical range does the heavy lifting, proving that the band has grown as much instrumentally as it has conceptually. While it’s expected that James Smith, lead vocalist and lyricist, still has plenty to say, it’s surprising how often this album lets the music do the talking first. Producer Justin Meldal-Johnsen (best known for his work with artists such as Beck, Nine Inch Nails, M83, Air, St. Vincent, Wolf Alice, and Deafheaven) gives Yard Act a broader palette than before, allowing pianos to wander through songs that might once have been driven solely by angular guitars, while rhythms flirt with funk, dance-rock, and a sense of forward motion to shoulder as much weight as Smith’s cynical storytelling. It’s the first record the four members wrote together in the same room, allowing their instinctive chemistry to shine through and feel noticeably more spontaneous than its predecessors. 

One of the album’s stronger pieces, “New Beginnings,” barrels forward with infectious confidence, anchored by muscular guitar work, driving drums, and a shout-along chorus. Everything locks together with effortless harmony. Smith’s repeated promise to “spend your winnings on new beginnings” isn’t his sharpest piece of writing, but it hardly matters when the band’s performance is this magnetic. It’s the kind of song that feels destined to become a live favorite because every element keeps pushing the next one forward.

The title track reaches that same destination through entirely different means; where “New Beginnings” surges, “You’re Gonna Need A Little Music” glides. Dreamy piano phrases weave around understated guitars and an unhurried rhythm section, creating the feeling of rolling down an open highway with nowhere particular to be. Even as the arrangement stays deceptively simple, subtle electronic textures keep the song in constant motion. Its refrain, reminding listeners that “you’re gonna need a little music,” plays with conviction instead of forced sentimentality. 


“Thrill of the Chase” is arguably the strongest track on the album and the most complete marriage of concept and execution. The sharp lyrics and title are embodied in the music itself, most notably in the relentless rhythm section, which reflects the endless pursuit of conformity and ambition that Smith criticizes. Its pounding rhythm section practically begs to soundtrack a getaway scene, but beneath that velocity lies some of Smith’s strongest writing: “Wish I never opened my eyes to what’s in front of me/pay the piper, don’t follow me/call the sniper, brand new policy/anybody tryna get ahead of the colony gets a bullet in their head before they get an apology.”

Aaron Lee Tasjan Listens to the Spirit of Todd Snider on ‘Get Over It, Underdog’
The singer-songwriter — and longtime friend of Snider, who died last year — champions those who perpetually have the deck stacked against them on an inspiring new album

By Marissa R. Moss

July 17, 2026
Aaron Lee Tasjan sought inspiration from the late Todd Snider for his new album 'Get Over It, Underdog.'
Aaron Lee Tasjan sought inspiration from the late Todd Snider for his new album ‘Get Over It, Underdog.’
Curtis Wayne Millard*

Everyone praises the outlaw — the outsider, the rebel. But what about the underdog? It’s a less glamorous character. Less marketable, for sure. But it’s where Aaron Lee Tasjan digs in on his newest LP, Get Over It, Underdog, with inspiration from his mentor and consummate booster of the underdog, the late Todd Snider.

Tasjan had reached an impasse after releasing 2024’s excellent Stellar Evolution and snagging a Grammy nomination. Despite any accolades that may have come, the environment for an independent artist seemed gloomier than ever. Being an underdog in entertainment kind of just felt like being underwater.

So Tasjan reached out to Snider, his close friend (Tasjan produced what would be Snider’s last album, High, Lonesome, and Then Some), for advice. He encouraged him — in his very Todd Snider sort of way — to keep going. Snider became a sounding board for a set of 11 songs that meld Tasjan’s knack for off-kilter but truly humanistic storytelling with vibrant melodies that shift and shine: a little glam rock, a little sing-talk folk, a lot of empathizing for the kind of underdog that may not get a lot of attention but still has a whole lot to say.

Tasjan has always excelled at connecting live. Like Snider, he embraces the space between the songs as much as the songs themselves. On Get Over It, Underdog, he draws you in immediately using those same tools of engagement. “Science Friction,” the album’s opener, finds him on a ramble through our own self-made state of discontent. “We can be pretty ugly and the world is round,” he sings, the strings letting everything sound a little more optimistic than we might actually deserve.

Lest things get predictable, Tasjan’s adept at letting the music battle with the lyrics in a way that hints at how any experience is far more complex than we are led to believe. “Lost & Alone” is driven by a chorus that is both peppy and Heartbreakers-esque, while still being pretty damn gloomy. And when Tasjan heads back home on “Ballad of an East Canton Lowlife,” he follows the kind of forgotten antihero who is far easier to dismiss than love. “I made it through the eighth grade,” he sings and slinks, “before I got a real job.”

Aaron Lee Tasjan Listens to the Spirit of Todd Snider on ‘Get Over It, Underdog’
The singer-songwriter — and longtime friend of Snider, who died last year — champions those who perpetually have the deck stacked against them on an inspiring new album

By Marissa R. Moss

July 17, 2026
Aaron Lee Tasjan sought inspiration from the late Todd Snider for his new album 'Get Over It, Underdog.'
Aaron Lee Tasjan sought inspiration from the late Todd Snider for his new album ‘Get Over It, Underdog.’
Curtis Wayne Millard*

Everyone praises the outlaw — the outsider, the rebel. But what about the underdog? It’s a less glamorous character. Less marketable, for sure. But it’s where Aaron Lee Tasjan digs in on his newest LP, Get Over It, Underdog, with inspiration from his mentor and consummate booster of the underdog, the late Todd Snider.

Tasjan had reached an impasse after releasing 2024’s excellent Stellar Evolution and snagging a Grammy nomination. Despite any accolades that may have come, the environment for an independent artist seemed gloomier than ever. Being an underdog in entertainment kind of just felt like being underwater.

So Tasjan reached out to Snider, his close friend (Tasjan produced what would be Snider’s last album, High, Lonesome, and Then Some), for advice. He encouraged him — in his very Todd Snider sort of way — to keep going. Snider became a sounding board for a set of 11 songs that meld Tasjan’s knack for off-kilter but truly humanistic storytelling with vibrant melodies that shift and shine: a little glam rock, a little sing-talk folk, a lot of empathizing for the kind of underdog that may not get a lot of attention but still has a whole lot to say.


Tasjan has always excelled at connecting live. Like Snider, he embraces the space between the songs as much as the songs themselves. On Get Over It, Underdog, he draws you in immediately using those same tools of engagement. “Science Friction,” the album’s opener, finds him on a ramble through our own self-made state of discontent. “We can be pretty ugly and the world is round,” he sings, the strings letting everything sound a little more optimistic than we might actually deserve.

Lest things get predictable, Tasjan’s adept at letting the music battle with the lyrics in a way that hints at how any experience is far more complex than we are led to believe. “Lost & Alone” is driven by a chorus that is both peppy and Heartbreakers-esque, while still being pretty damn gloomy. And when Tasjan heads back home on “Ballad of an East Canton Lowlife,” he follows the kind of forgotten antihero who is far easier to dismiss than love. “I made it through the eighth grade,” he sings and slinks, “before I got a real job.”

Humor here, is key, but mixed with a real-world sort of sadness that only someone like Tasjan — and Snider — could bring. “She goes harder than Bea Arthur, she’s the golden one,” Tasjan sings on “Lydia’s Boots,” a song written after a Lydia Loveless concert. It’s partly an ode to the magic of a stage performance (and some great shoes), but there’s also a melancholy desire to dream and disappear in the life of anyone else. 

Snider died before Tasjan could complete the album, and both his presence and his loss is woven throughout. On the tender piano ballad “The Dream Comes True” that closes Get Over It, Underdog, Tasjan is making amends with what it means to chase what you love, even when it sometimes leaves you broken: how it’s the little moments with the friends you can lean on that shine brighter than any marquee.

Saint Slayyyter
It’s the American Dream, Slayyyter Edition: Flop, flop again, then tap into your inner Cookie Monster Pajama Pants Girl and make a trash-pop masterpiece. While rifling through feathers and fringe, Catherine Grace Garner told us all about the making of WOR$T GIRL IN AMERICA, her follow-up album on the way, and her jagged, 2026-as-hell path to pop stardom.
By Walden Green
Photography by Bowen Fernie
August 10, 2026
Image may contain Face Head Person Photography Portrait Fashion Accessories Jewelry Necklace Blonde and Hair


When she released each of her first two studio albums, Catherine Grace Garner got a tattoo. She has “Paradise,” for her 2021 debut Troubled Paradise, in gothic lettering across the knuckles of her right hand, and “Starfucker” scrawled like an A-lister’s hasty signature on her left inner forearm. Seated across from the artist better known as Slayyyter at a chain coffeeshop in Midtown Manhattan, I ask if she’s gotten any ink to commemorate WOR$T GIRL IN AMERICA, the trashy, clashy new album that was, at one point, supposed to be her last. “No, now I have a superstition,” the 29-year-old Missouri native explains, her bleached curls—she’s keeping the roots dark these days—framing her face in frazzled tresses, “that that makes albums flop. I’ll never get another album tattoo.”

What does it mean to flop? “Often psychic death can inspire a new acceptance of man’s fate,” Pitchfork contributor Meaghan Garvey writes in her new memoir Midwestern Death Trip. “What you thought was the end is just the beginning.” Zara Larsson says, “I’ve had success, and then my flop era, and then it’s like, Well, OK, I’ve flopped, so it doesn’t scare me anymore.” WGIA$—that’s how Garner stylizes it—is a chronicle of successive flop eras: bad boyfriends, albums that didn’t land the way she hoped they would, addiction, abandonment, suicide. It has made her orders of magnitude more famous than she’s ever been before.

Garner arrives at our shoot at Mood Fabrics in June directly from an early morning Grammys event, where she sang a stripped-down, acoustic cover of the WGIA$ single “CRANK” (if you’ve heard the original “CRANK,” this is very funny). The awards-baiting moves would’ve been unfathomable six months ago. The month prior, she’d performed on late-night TV for the first time, bedecked in silver fringe as she sang “DANCE…” on the Tonight Show stage. “I don’t feel like a pop star,” Garner says, looking more and more like one by the day. “If I’m a pop star I’m not a very good one, because I feel like I run around and I scream and my hair’s always gross.”

Image may contain Body Part Finger Hand Person Face Head Photography Portrait Adult and Fashion
The pop stars Garner grew up with in the late ’90s and early aughts were the products and, often, victims of exacting major-label machinery. But over the last two decades, the serrated steel of the star machine has rusted, forcing young artists to effectively develop themselves. So Slayyyter’s a pop star, yes, but also her own creative director and, occasionally, an auteur filmmaker. Garner directed four out of WGIA$’s five proper music videos on her own, and makes most of her stage costumes. “It’s all glue,” she winks. She carefully considered every aspect of the album’s packaging, down to where the side-A, side-B split—between “YES GODDD” and “UNKNOWN LOVERZ”—would fall on vinyl copies: “It’s such a needle scratch of the hardest electronic song, and then you flip it and it’s like, burn you a CD,” Garner coos, slipping up a half-octave into an uncanny Britney Spears.

After several years spent uploading lo-fi laptop pop to SoundCloud while working a day job as a hair salon receptionist, Hurricane Slayyyter broke containment into the gay internet with 2018’s “BFF,” a Juicy Couture joyride with a beat she bought off the Tumblr-infamous Ayesha Erotica. So Garner adopted a tits-forward, Y2K-throwback aesthetic, not because it felt integral to her artistic ambitions but because “you have to do what you have to do until you can do what you want,” and sucked it up when her music was called “hyperpop,” which she calls a “horrible micro-term.”

Garner felt no urgency to leave St. Louis, but the choice was made for her when COVID hit while she was in Los Angeles taking meetings with songwriters. In an apartment in Beverly Hills, she began work on the songs that would become Troubled Paradise, which came out on the FADER Label. The album was scattershot in both quality and vision—Garner has since called it “a sack of shit”—and tainted by the arduous process of recording over Zoom. 2023’s Starfucker zeroed in on a caricature of a bottle-blonde, surged-up Hollywood starlet, but its Swarovski synth pop lacked a distinct personality. By then, Garner was already thinking about quitting music. She confesses, “I felt so depressed during that time that I was like, Maybe it’s my last album because I don't wanna be here anymore.”

Image may contain Adult Person Face and Head
Image may contain Adult Person Hair Curly Hair Tape Accessories Jewelry and Necklace
The stakes for WGIA$ were, therefore, both very high and very low. That gave Garner licence to tap into her inner “Cookie Monster Pajama Pants Girl,” an image that will be instantaneously evocative to anyone who attended public high school during either of the Obama administrations. Her term of art for the album’s collective influences is “iPod music,” which ties it to a particular era as well as a decidedly personal approach to taste and curation. After all, “with iPods, you had to be so specific about what music you wanted to listen to at school that day.” Portions of WGIA$ are like Rorschach inkblot tests for your graduation year. Class of 2008? “GAS STATION” is guaranteed to induce acid flashbacks of the first Crystal Castles LP. 2012? “$T LOSER” is a Death Grips song. 2007? “$T LOSER” is a SebastiAn song.

WGIA$ was born of Tumblr blogs and iTunes Free Singles of the Week. “I’m an internet girl,” Garner declares, and she spends the rest of our conversation proving it, quoting niche memes, (“You already know it’s a whole ass mood”), opining on Baddies USA (“The only real reality TV show left”), and namechecking Gen Z Rachel Dolezal-turned-downtown lit scene darling Woah Vicky. “I’m very anti-social media break, too. You miss things, you know? You miss culture.” There’s something thrilling to Garner about the public narrative around her slipping beyond her grasp. “I think about you, do you think about me?” she jeers, cajoles, pleads on “$T LOSER,” a thundering battle cry for the slackers and deadbeats of her hometown.

Existing online, in public and semi-public fashion, for more than half her life has left Garner with more baggage than, say, your average Disney alum. In 2019, a collection of her old tweets that made repeated use of the N-word began circulating online. They’d been posted to a long-defunct One Direction and Fifth Harmony stan account under the username @camilkacowbello in 2012 and 2013, when Garner was around 15. Her apology took the form of a handwritten letter, in which she wrote, “I wish so badly I could beat the shit out of my 15 year old self for behaving so inappropriately.”

Image may contain Adult Person Shop Face and Head
The double-edged sword of having unfettered internet access long before she had a fully formed cerebral cortex: Garner now possesses a near-instinctual knack for virality. The first moment I knew that WGIA$ was going to be a moment, I was in a huddle of gay guys all pogoing to “CRANK.” The song had dropped days before, but there was one line that everyone already knew: “He wanna fuck Slayyyter, Richard we should link later.” “I was trying to be the GIF of the pen on fire,” Garner says about that lyric, which works not just as double-, but triple-entendre; “@Slayyyter” was her first social media handle, borrowed from Rory Cochrane’s Ron Slater, the prodigious stoner in Richard Linklater’s Dazed and Confused.

Garner seems to have an easier time describing her music by way of filmmakers—Linklater, David Lynch, Harmony Korine—than other recording artists. Most of the WGIA$ sessions started with vision boards instead of demos. One day, Garner showed up to the studio with a knockoff Chanel purse, its leather “all cracked and gross,” wrapped in an Aurel Schmidt x Supreme scarf emblazoned with trash and weed nuggets and dime baggies. Let’s make a song that sounds like this, she told producers Wyatt Bernard and Austin Corona. That song would end up as WGIA$’s lead single, “BEAT UP CHANEL$,” alternately as sticky-sweet as resin and as caustic as tar.

Image may contain Face Head Person Photography Portrait Accessories Jewelry Necklace Clothing Swimwear and Adult
And like a filmmaker, Garner’s visual language is replete with recurring motifs, totems of Midwestern maximalism: bunny rabbits, American flags, firearms, baseball bats, a fascinator that looks like a whole taxidermied swan. On the day of our interview, she’s sporting a pair of black boots from the “CRANK” shoot, meticulously painted to appear mud-splattered. Then there’s the WGIA$ logo, a red dollar sign inscribed in a circle, reminiscent of the modern “branded everything” style adopted by luxury fashion houses like Balenciaga and Louis Vuitton. When Garner revealed the album cover, the response from fans was decidedly mixed. “It kind of annoys you at first,” she admits. “You’re like, No trust me. You don’t know, but I know, and I wish that you knew. Let me help you know.” I tempt her to get catty by positing that every pop stan on the internet right now thinks they’re a creative director, but she doesn’t take the bait. “I kind of love it. Maybe everybody is.”

From Mood, Garner leads our crew a block over to Hai Trim & Feathers, an embellishments shop where she’s a regular patron. It’s here she bought the lilac ostrich feathers that crowned her head during a recent performance at Gov Ball. Now she’s eyeing some more feathers, these ones dip-dyed in ombré tones, as she laments how contemporary album “eras” have been reduced to “Party City themes.” For WGIA$, she decided that her theme would be growing up in St. Louis, because she couldn’t imagine anyone throwing a party about that.

A couple years ago, Garner got a Jesus piece done by the tattoo artist Mark Mahoney for an Interview Magazine feature. Showing it off on her bicep, she tells me about attending Catholic school, where she watched self-professed Christians belittle her family for being poor. Her father was also a frequent subject of church gossip back in St. Louis, for his drinking habits and his chronic unemployment. In WGIA$’s penultimate moments, Garner recites the Prayer of St. Francis: “To understand rather than be understood/To love than be loved/For it is by self-forgetting that one finds/It is by forgiving that one is forgiven.”

Image may contain Blonde Hair Person Adult Accessories Jewelry Necklace Face and Head
The way Garner sometimes speaks about her career, you might think she was a medieval flagellant marching from hamlet to hamlet. “Touring is my biggest love,” she proclaims. “I love living on a bus and getting scrappy, when my back starts to hurt or my knees are all bloody.” There’s a near-religious fervor, too, in how pop stans sing the praises of their chosen divas. She’s carrying pop music on her back. That’s mother. When Garner wails the title of “YES GODDD,” distortion blowing her voice to smithereens, she is speaking two languages: that of religious exaltation, and that of AAVE filtered down through gay Twitter. She doesn’t want to be worshipped, but Garner does see her live shows as a sort of divine communion. “It feels like I’m preaching,” she says.

Garner’s nonlinear, piecemeal writing process allowed her to discover what these songs were about gradually. “GAS STATION” languished in the darkwave murk of a breakup until she realized she was actually singing about her dad: “You have shitty exes because your first relationship with a man was shitty.” The music video for “DANCE…” opens with an altercation between Garner and an older, bespectacled man. She bars herself in her room, aims a shotgun at the door, and blam. Thirteen tracks later, Chekov’s shotgun returns. “Tell them I am sorry for the gun inside my bedroom drawer,” Garner monotones on album closer “BRITTANY MURPHY,” each word like a bit of buckshot to the gut. The most loaded—literally—lyric on WGIA$ bridges the guilt of suicidal ideation, something Garner has publicly struggled with, and the guilt of career suicide. She “hates the thought of teaching young, sweet kids who come to my shows, You spent all this time supporting me and fuck that.”

Image may contain Face Head Person Photography Portrait Accessories Bag Handbag Jewelry Necklace Adult and City
“BRITTANY MURPHY”’s profoundly feel-bad chorus reconstructs a familiar horror scene. It’s the blurry end of a night out, and Garner just can’t seem to find her jacket. She stumbles around, accosts the bouncer, and ruminates on her eventual funeral: “Is my face too disgusting for open casket?” WGIA$ is a druggy album that is lucid about the ramifications of doing a lot of drugs. “I feel as if I’m making a caricature of the kind of person people have seen me as, which is this sloppy, drunk, tweaky girl,” says Garner, who got sober for the first time a few years back, though it didn’t fully stick. “It’s hazy what my relationship with alcohol is at this age or point, but I’m just not a person who’s ever been able to control themselves.”

These days, Garner is “diving back into sobriety again, or at least trying to.” She’s also getting better at directing her wrath toward those more deserving. By now it’s common knowledge that there’s a companion album in the works, tentatively titled WOR$T MAN IN AMERICA—a tribute to her most recent ex, who Garner discovered was cheating on her just two days before the release of WGIA$. Actually, Garner prefers to call it an “inverse album,” citing a recently popular meme format wherein one dreams up an “evil” version of an artist’s discography. For example, “CANNIBALISM!” becomes “VEGANISM!” “BEAT UP CHANEL$” become “brand new chanel$.”

“I wonder if you got her in your room, I call you on the phone to start a fight…but I’m here standing still,” Garner wails on “brand new chanel$,” which dropped at the end of July. You can hear her frame of nostalgia shifting in real time, into the arena synth-rock of M83 and Madeon’s oops-all-drops EDM. (Slayyyter features on the latter’s new album, Victory.) She knows she’ll have even bigger rooms to fill pretty soon. Garner has no ire, though, for the other woman. “I never judge the drunkest girl at the party,” she vows. “That person has torture in their heart. Go give them a shoulder to cry on.” Heed her words. To flop is, after all, to open the door to slay; “it is in dying that we are born to eternal life.” And that girl might one day pick herself off the bathroom floor, rinse the vomit from her hair, and make all of her American dreams come true.

Tamsin Greig and Nicky Spence rescue Weber’s Oberon at the Proms
The composer’s nonsensical last opera has been scrubbed up – plus the best of this year’s Proms

    
Ivan Hewett
 Classical Music Critic. 
Neil McCormick
 Chief Music Critic. 
Nicholas Kenyon
 Chief Opera Critic

Published 27 July 2026 3:14pm BST

Tamsin Greig narrated this new version of Oberon at the Proms
Tamsin Greig narrated this new version of Oberon at the Proms Credit: Chris Christodoulou
With 86 concerts ranging from Elizabethan song to blockbuster symphonies to hardcore modernism, the Proms’ programme seems mouth-watering on paper. But does every Prom always live up to the hype? Here are the recommendations and latest reviews from The Telegraph’s critics.

This article will be updated on a rolling basis throughout the Proms.

Our experts’ reviews:

First Night of the Proms
Oberon ★★★★☆
The plot of Carl Maria von Weber’s last opera Oberon, which premiered 200 years ago, is a farrago of nonsense. It is not the story of Shakespeare’s A Midsummer Night’s Dream but rather a libretto by Planché about a quarrelling Oberon and Titania, who will only reconcile if a human couple prove that they can be faithful in love. It whisks us from fairy glades to Arabian nights, following the love-quest of a French knight for the daughter of the Caliph of Baghdad. Yes, it’s a vacuous scenario, but it inspires some wonderful music. Scrubbed up for the Proms by experienced Weber conductor Mark Elder and the Orchestre Révolutionnaire et Romantique, Oberon emerged as a wonderfully inventive opera, if still unsatisfactory.

The latest rescue attempt in the opera’s chequered history, this new version foregrounded the music and replaced all the tiresome dialogue with Timothy Knapman’s witty new verse narration, delivered with verve by actress Tasmin Greig (Friday Night Dinner). Knapman was not able to resist arch references to Shakespeare’s play (“Lord, what fools these mortals be”).

The fine Monteverdi Choir brought plenty of energy but, in Emma Doherty’s semi-staging, there was slightly too much movement on a stage crowded with an orchestra; Greig was called upon to repeatedly circumnavigate the platform, while Nicky Spence’s commanding, incisive Oberon sang from above and then came to the front of the stage to imperiously order the others around.

Nicky Spence was commanding and incisive in the title role in Oberon
Nicky Spence was commanding and incisive in the title role in Oberon Credit: Chris Christodoulou
Still, it kept our attention while we waited for the next ravishing musical number. Right from Anneke Scott’s first eloquent solo horn call, which launches the famous Overture, Elder sculpted precisely the old sounds of twittering woodwind and blazing, open-voiced brass, so refreshingly different from their modern equivalents.

As the ever-faithful knight Sir Huon, John Findon tossed off his virtuosic roulades, though with more heroism than lyricism. There was good support from Yannick Debus as his squire Sherasmin especially in the fine Act Two Quartet. I didn’t much warm to the steely soprano of Jennifer Davis as the princess Reiza, who has the opera’s big set-piece scene, Ocean, thou mighty monster, in the midst of the brilliant, choppy shipwreck scene of Act Two. Her cooler Cavatina Mourn thou, poor heart in Act Three was touching.

The standout Prom debut of the evening was Rachael Wilson as Fatima, Reiza’s attendant, a lovely, straight-edged voice which really responded to the sounds of the old instruments. As Puck, J’Nai Bridges was cheerful, and Charlotte Bowden and Jessica Cale were two rapturous mermaids. I hope, though I am not overly optimistic, that they all get a chance to repeat these demanding roles. NK

Aurora Orchestra ★★★★☆
True to its namesake, the Roman goddess of the dawn, the eternally young and fresh-faced Aurora Orchestra aims to be a new dawn for orchestral music. The players forswear the comfort and ease of seats and music stands. They stand to play, and sometimes even perambulate, making ever-changing choreographed patterns that mirror the music’s form. They perform from memory, as does their conductor and founder Nicholas Collon. And in their recent Proms appearances the orchestra has prefaced their performance of some big-boned masterpiece with the dramatised story of the work’s creation, mingling biography with some deft musical analysis.

All this we got last night. The masterpiece that filled the second half of Aurora’s Prom was the first symphony of Gustav Mahler. Before that we learnt from Collon and the players how cuckoo-calls and military marches and swaying Viennese country dances are woven into the music. Mezzo-soprano Natalie Lewis sang a couple of Mahler’s songs that he later incorporated into the symphony, with lovely fragile tenderness.

This was all helpful and illuminating. The portrayal of Mahler himself as played by Jack Bardoe was less so. He rampaged around the platform, raging against dim-witted critics who panned the first performance, explained to a bewildered friend how he wanted to embrace the whole world in his music, and frantically scribbled out a detailed listening guide for the symphony which he later tore up. It made Mahler into a comic caricature of the egotistical romantic artist, which didn’t seem the best advertisement for the piece we were about to hear.

But the moment the symphony began, with those extraordinary glistening sounds evoking the stillness of a forest at first light, Mahler and his troubles were forgotten. It was impressive to see all those players playing such a vast piece from memory, and it would be easy to imagine that the performance had a special edge-of-the-seat urgency. What actually came across was a special intensity of listening, each player responding to cues in a way that made the interconnections in Mahler’s rich web of sound feel especially vivid. You could feel one group of players ‘handing over’ a melody to another.

Beyond that, the performance under Collon’s clean and unfussy direction was lucid and well-paced with some beautiful, depth-of-the-forest sounds from the eight horns. Here and there it seemed under-characterised – the tipsy “village band” moment could have been more uproarious, the bird-calls more wild – but the ecstatic ending certainly hit the nail on the head. IH

Listen to this Prom for 70 days via BBC Sounds.

Olivier Latry plays Bach ★★★★★
When Olivier Latry appeared for his solo Prom concert on a Sunday morning, he was sporting a silver lamé jacket. Not the outfit you might expect for an all-Bach organ concert from a cathedral organist, but then Latry isn’t your typical specimen of the breed. One of the Notre-Dame’s four titular organists since 1985, he’s a globe-trotting superstar, whose recent recording of Bach organ music Bach to the Future, was startlingly bold and bright in a way that could win over even the most hardened organ sceptics.

This enthralling 90-minute concert, given in a packed and fervently enthusiastic Royal Albert Hall, could surely have done the same. Latry’s programme was cleverly chosen to portray Bach not as a provincial German Protestant but as a fabricator of timeless music visions, needing only some imaginative recolouring and dancing rhythmic energy to throw off the dust of centuries.

Four of the pieces were transcriptions and arrangements of Bach’s music, done by Latry’s forebears in the French romantic organ tradition that now stretches back almost 200 years. Among them was Henri Messerer’s arrangement of the Chaconne for solo violin, which added a mystical fervour to the severe majesty of Bach’s original. Later came Maurice Duruflé’s arrangement of the chorale we know as Jesu Joy of Man’s Desiring, which can sometimes sound sentimental but didn’t in this beautiful performance.

Olivier Latry has been one of four titular organists of the Great Organ of Notre-Dame Cathedral since 1985
Olivier Latry has been one of four titular organists of the Great Organ of Notre-Dame Cathedral since 1985 Credit: Luc Castel
After all this meditative tenderness Latry blew away the smell of incense with an astonishingly vigorous, buoyant performance of Bach’s arrangement of Vivaldi’s concerto for two violins. He then threw off the famous Toccata and Fugue in D minor beloved of horror film directors with a rhetorical flourish that made it seem interestingly different, more grand and imperious than spooky.

You could say all this was playing to the gallery – and why shouldn’t he? – but Latry showed he could be subtle too, with a performance of the Chorale Kommst du Nun, Jesu. The three interwoven, dancing melodies (played by left and right hand and on the pedals) were as poised and perfect as a pas de trois in a ballet. Like all good showmen, Latry saved the most spectacular thing until last: an improvisation on a four-note pattern derived from the name “Bach”. The towering harmonies and torrents of musical lava he conjured from this apparently dull pattern were simply extraordinary. They evoked those mighty visions of the future conjured by dreamers of a bygone era like H.G Wells and Jules Verne. “Bach to the Future” indeed. IH

Sueye Park and the BBC Philharmonic ★★★★☆
Sueye Park brought an intimate radiance to Sibelius's Violin Concerto
Sueye Park brought an intimate radiance to Sibelius’s Violin Concerto Credit: BBC/ Chris Christodoulou
Given that all the concerts are free to listen to online, the Proms offer a chance for an orchestra to shine on a wider stage, and the BBC Philharmonic Orchestra has certainly seized that opportunity. Following their performance of Beethoven’s Ninth (see below), they returned the next day with a UK premiere from a soon-to-be centenarian composer and Richard Strauss’s most earth-shaking tone-poem.

In between they gave a platform to the most recent winner of the Sibelius Violin Competition Sueye Park, who played the Violin Concerto by Sibelius that some say is the hardest in the repertoire. Many performers take the view they have to dominate the orchestra, which is a challenge – Sibelius’s orchestra often sounds as massive and tumultuous as a glacier in a midnight storm. The 26-year-old South Korean violinist didn’t try, in fact her sound often had a remarkable delicacy. But she soared above the orchestra nonetheless, partly because she refused to let the music rush her. Everything, especially the sad, lonely cadenzas (solo spots) had a meditative spaciousness, and overall the concerto glowed with a special intimate radiance.

Some of the credit for this marvel must go to conductor John Storgårds, who was a very tactful accompanist without appearing to hold the orchestra back. In Tales of a Summer Sea, a musical seascape by the French-American composer Betsy Jolas (who turns 100 in two weeks), the orchestra could really let rip. The piece is almost half a century old, and it has the abrasive modernism of much French music of that era.

The BBC Philharmonic Orchestra brought drama and colour to the evening
The BBC Philharmonic Orchestra brought drama and colour to the evening Credit: BBC/ Chris Christodoulou,
Unlike Debussy’s La Mer (the Sea), which is all glitter and spray, Jolas’s piece dives down to the sea’s depths. The pedal drums roared and swooped like primeval beasts, the basses groaned, cymbals hissed. In the programme, Jolas writes that her piece was inspired by memories of being nearly washed overboard in the Atlantic in 1940; this piece gave the impression she quite enjoyed it.

After that celebration of nature’s power came Strauss’s hymn of praise to Nietzsche’s Übermensch, Also Sprach Zarathustra, the piece made famous in Stanley Kubrick’s 2001: A Space Odyssey and satirised in Barbie. It’s a piece well suited to the Proms, thanks to the Royal Albert Hall’s tremendous organ and the fact that the deep bell that launches the final Song of the Night Wanderer can be placed way up in the galleries. If the mysterious ending wasn’t quite as immaculate as it needs to be, the previous journey lacked nothing in colour and drama. IH

Listen to this Prom for 30 days on BBC Sounds. Watch it on BBC Four on Aug 7

Abel Selaocoe and the BBC Philharmonic ★★★★☆
Abel Selaocoe brought poignancy to a new cello concerto at the Proms
Abel Selaocoe brought warmth and poignancy to Jessie Montgomery’s new cello concerto at the BBC Proms Credit: Chris Christodoulou
Could anything steal the thunder from Beethoven’s immortal Ninth Symphony? Impossible, you might think. But the BBC Philharmonic’s performance of the Ninth was almost overshadowed by what came before: a South African cellist performing a brand-new cello concerto by African American composer Jessie Montgomery.

Abel Selaocoe is a star musician, well known for blending improvisation inspired by South African songs with the works of classical composers such as Bach. Montgomery, who often brings themes of social justice and Black history into her music, wrote this concerto for him. Co-commissioned by the BBC, the piece is based on three poems about memory and protest by Montgomery’s mother, the late playwright Robbie McCauley.

All of which might lead you to expect a mood of defiance. In fact, the concerto, subtitled These Righteous Paths, was striking in its delicacy and generosity of spirit. There was an echo of African call-and-response in the opening flourish from Selaocoe, answered by the orchestral brass. As the slow opening movement unwound, the woodwind’s florid melodies were answered by Selaocoe in a way that was both meditative and ecstatic, evoking age-old folk traditions. When he gently broke into African song, it felt entirely natural.

Abel Selaocoe performing with the BBC Philharmonic at the Proms
Abel Selaocoe performs with the BBC Philharmonic at the BBC Proms Credit: Chris Christodoulou
The second movement was a virtuoso dance, in which Selaocoe’s melody pirouetted at the very top of the cello’s range; the third, a glowingly spacious, meditative finale. His rhapsodic singing, sometimes deep like a didgeridoo, sometimes high and dreaming, brought the concerto to a close.

It left behind a sense of glowing ease, rudely shattered after the interval by the strenuous aspiration of Beethoven’s Ninth – especially strenuous in this performance, as the conductor Gianandrea Noseda drove the first two movements at a blistering pace. The performance seemed to skim over the music rather than engage with it, but my spirits were lifted by the graceful lyricism of the third movement and the drama of the fourth. The transition from the orchestra’s wordless “speaking” into real song was tellingly shaped. The four soloists were variable in quality, but the Philharmonia Chorus were on blazing form.

All in all, the performance felt patchy, but the evening as a whole was hugely nourishing. We were offered two visions of the way human beings can reach for transcendence in music (plus, as a curtain-raiser, Bach’s Fantasia and Fugue in G minor for organ in Andrew Davis’s richly coloured orchestral arrangement). Not many concerts can boast that. IH

Listen to this Prom for 30 days on BBC Sounds

Prog Rock: A Fanfare for the Common Man ★★★★☆
Carl Palmer
Carl Palmer’s performance injects thumping rock energy and volume into proceedings Credit: Andy Paradise
What would “prog rock” sound like… without the rock? That was the question underlying this peculiar yet entertaining concert as Prog went to the Proms. What was once both lauded and derided as Britain’s most adventurous, pretentious and all-encompassing highbrow popular music genre was rendered as a gentle evening of nostalgic easy listening.

Amiable BBC 6 Music DJ Stuart Maconie programmed an ambitious setlist that covered a wide span of the progressive genre that flourished here from 1969 to 1977, before crashing into disrepute under the disdainful attack of punk barbarians.

Guided by the expressive baton of long-haired, guest conductor Robert Ames, the 66-strong BBC Concert Orchestra delivered lush interpretations of prog classics from Genesis, King Crimson, Pink Floyd, Jethro Tull, Supertramp and Soft Machine. Yet without all the sustained guitars, sound warping synths, powerhouse drums and dazzling lasers, music that once sounded almost transgressively experimental was revealed to be mostly melodious and genteel. We were treated to an excerpt from the floridly complex 1972 Yes album Close to the Edge that couldn’t have got further from the edge if it parked in the middle of the road.

Conductor Robert Ames guided the orchestra through interpretations of prog classics from Genesis to Pink Floyd
Conductor Robert Ames guided the orchestra through interpretations of prog classics from Genesis to Pink Floyd Credit: Andy Paradise
Guest vocalist Jane Weaver sang high, sweet versions of Northern Lights by Renaissance and Nights in White Satin by The Moody Blues that made you wonder whether Burt Bacharach was a secret prog fan. Guy Garvey from Elbow sang selections from early Genesis that emphasised the pastoral folksiness of their melodies. His version of King Crimson’s I Talk to the Wind gilded by ethereal harmonies from The Staves had a flowing beauty that brought to mind John Barry’s easy listening classic Born Free, while pumped-up horns on 21st Century Schizoid Man turned it into a swaggering prog Bond theme.

Many of the pieces seemed a bit aimless now that they were removed from the ambitiously conceptual framework of the originals. The vocals of Gruff Rhys of Super Furry Animals were rather too tentative and insipid on a version of The Incredible String Band’s Maker of Islands. Without Pink Floyd’s gravitas, The Great Gig in the Sky was in danger of turning into a harmonious yet unadventurous two-chord orchestral vamp until Vanessa Haynes lifted it all the way to the dark side of the moon with her note perfect recreation of the originals extemporised operatic soul vocal. “They said they were gonna let me do that,” Maconie joked as Haynes went off to thunderous applause.

Guest vocalist Jane Weaver
Guest vocalist Jane Weaver sang versions of Northern Lights by Renaissance and Nights in White Satin by The Moody Blues
The orchestra sounded fantastic, and all the arrangements were interesting, even if all those elaborate lyrics, tricky time signatures, tempo and style switches made prog sound like an eccentric offshoot of operetta. I am not sure those were the classical heights the original prog combatants were aiming for.

Thank goodness, then, for veteran drummer Carl Palmer, who came on for two pieces by his pioneering trio Emerson, Lake and Palmer, and immediately injected some thumping rock energy and volume into proceedings. “One, two, three…” he shouted, assertively taking over from the conductor to lead the orchestra into a bombastic orchestral version of ELP’s 1970 rock interpretation of an 1874 piano suite by Mussorgsky. I am not sure how progressive all of this could claim to be, but it was tremendous fun. NMC

Available for 85 days on BBC Sounds

First Night of the Proms ★★★★☆
The evenings are just beginning to shorten, Wimbledon and the World Cup have passed, the grass is starting to look parched. You could almost think the best of the summer is over. But for classical music lovers it’s just starting. The Proms have launched what will be a brilliant sunburst of music extending for eight weeks right through to the autumn. There are 86 concerts, including 20 new pieces, great international orchestras, stellar soloists. It’s a feast, as always.

For the opening Prom, the BBC Symphony Orchestra gave us a taster of the American theme that runs through this season, celebrating the 250th anniversary of American independence. It could have been the moment to celebrate the radical spirit in American music, but perhaps wisely the Proms programmers decided to save that for later in the season. Instead, the evening kicked off with Aaron Copland’s mighty evocation of the democratic spirit of the nation, in his Fanfare for the Common Man. In the huge space of the Royal Albert Hall it was as colossal and awe-inspiring as the Brooklyn Bridge.

In the huge space of the Royal Albert Hall, Aaron Copland's Fanfare for the Common Man was as colossal as the Brooklyn Bridge
In the huge space of the Royal Albert Hall, Aaron Copland’s Fanfare for the Common Man was as colossal as the Brooklyn Bridge Credit: Chris Christodoulou/BBC
Then came the irrepressible high spirits of George Gershwin’s An American in Paris, captured to perfection by the orchestra and the evening’s astonishingly energised conductor Dalia Stasevska. One waits for that wonderfully open-hearted trumpet melody with keen anticipation, and Philip Cobb did not disappoint. He made it seem tipsy, grand and sentimental, all at once.

George Gershwin's An American in Paris was captured to perfection by Stasevska
George Gershwin’s An American in Paris was captured to perfection by Stasevska Credit: Chris Christodoulou/BBC
To follow that with Ravel’s Piano Concerto was hardly a jolt. By the time he came to compose the piece, Ravel had fallen under Gershwin’s spell, and in a good performance its combination of American sassiness and Ravel’s own exquisite French sensibility has a peculiar magic. Unfortunately, this performance from young superstar Korean pianist Yunchan Lim was disappointing. In the fast outer movements, his agile, sensitive hands seemed to dance around the music rather than engaging with it. In the slow movement, Lim’s odd veering between almost-not-there lightness and sudden surges of expressive warmth robbed the famous long melody of its spun-steel strength.


The American theme persisted in the evening’s new piece, That the sunrise not leave us unmoved, a setting of fragments of poems by Emily Dickinson by young British composer Josephine Stephenson. Finding a musical analogue to Dickinson’s intimate but blazing words is a challenge, but Stephenson rose to it. Sometimes we would hear words from the BBC Singers and Chorus in tones of fluttering but tentative urgency, haloed by the orchestra in not-quite definite harmonies. Then suddenly the full organ and orchestra would blaze out to mark a moment of illumination before subsiding back to uncertainty. It was affecting, and well worth hearing again.

The BBC Singers and Chorus hit the right note with each piece they sang
The BBC Singers and Chorus hit the right note with each piece they sang Credit: Chris Christodoulou/BBC
Finally came something very English: a setting of Edmund Blunden’s poem For St Cecilia by early 20th-century composer Gerald Finzi. The tenor soloist Thomas Atkins had just the right English choral scholar sound, the BBC Singers and Chorus were on fine lusty form, and the orchestra beautifully caught the work’s surprisingly military-sounding grandeur. If I wasn’t persuaded that the work is a neglected masterpiece, it certainly wasn’t the performers’ fault.

Of course, it wouldn’t be the First Night without a surprise or two, and sure enough the evening ended with a full-bodied rendition of Oasis’s Wonderwall. A lament, perhaps, rather than a sign of hope given Wednesday’s World Cup semi-final results, but a crowd-pleasing Proms moment nonetheless. IH

Review: Bruckner – Symphony No. 4 (“Romantic”) – Atlanta Symphony Orchestra, Nathalie Stutzmann
David A. McConnell - August 2, 2026

Impressed by this partnership’s Dvořák Ninth (review), I was eager to hear their Bruckner fourth, surely the composer’s most popular symphony both in the concert hall and on record. This new reading, while impressive, is not a complete success.


Symphony No. 4
Amazon
Presto
Apple
This reading lacks the instrumental weight and depth of color that I would argue makes for an authentic Bruckner sound. Having grown up listening to many Telarc recordings of this orchestra, I lay the blame for this deficiency on the engineering. The mics are placed too close to the musicians, surely to minimize audience noise during this live recording. There is little bloom to the sound, and no real hall ambience. Climaxes feel contained, and softer dynamics are limited by the proximity of the mics. Bruckner’s music was written with a warm, reverberant acoustic in mind, and that acoustic is an integral part of the sound. Not so here.

Instead, it is like standing one foot away from Seurat’s A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte: every brush stroke is visible, and we might appreciate how the painting is assembled, but it comes at the cost of the image’s beauty, mystery and expression.

The second issue has to do with the edition. There are now ten different editions of the fourth symphony to choose from. Stutzmann uses the late Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs’ 2021 critical edition of the 1878–81 version, which has been recorded only once before, by Simon Rattle with the LSO (LSO Live). For that album Cohrs described his edition as “the second version of the ‘Romantic’ Symphony as it stood by the end of 1881,” including “Bruckner’s own suggested cut in the Finale.” But Stutzmann does not take the cut — why not? Why embrace a little-known version and then ignore one of its most significant, composer-sanctioned changes? The liner notes offer no discussion of the edition, nor is it listed on either side of the booklet cover.

As to the performance itself, I had initially intended to limit comparisons to other American recordings, but it now seems best to set Stutzmann against Rattle (details – review). His performance is faster, with an overall timing of 61’30” against Stutzmann’s 71’39”, and the largest difference comes in the final movement, where Stutzmann restores the passage Bruckner marked for cutting. Each conductor belongs to a different interpretive school. Rattle recalls Karajan: a subtle use of rubato, polished orchestral sound with resplendent brass and gossamer strings, a unified pulse that keeps the structure in view. Stutzmann is far closer to Jochum: much freer rubato, a more heterogeneous orchestral sound and, more distinctively, an intensely songful rendering of Bruckner’s score.

Nathalie Stutzmann conducting the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra
Nathalie Stutzmann conducts the Atlanta Symphony Orchestra. Photo © Rand Lines.

Stutzmann’s approach is compelling in the second movement, though at 17’05” it feels more Adagio than Bruckner’s Andante quasi Allegretto. In the finale the flexibility sometimes compromises a sense of the overarching structure, and there are moments when the energy level dips precipitously low. This is less of an issue in the opening movement, where one does get to hear much of the contrapuntal inner voices.

Both Rattle and Stutzmann solicit rounded articulation, and I often missed the cut and thrust of Blomstedt’s San Francisco account on Decca and Honeck’s Pittsburgh performance on Reference, especially in the Scherzo. Both drive the music harder, encouraging more wildness in the trumpet-capped climaxes. Bruckner described the Trio as “how a barrel-organ plays during the midday meal in the forest.” Honeck is perhaps best at capturing this rustic picture, even if he is reticent to allow an earthier, less refined sound. Stutzmann conjures a poised, lightly sprung elegance that I enjoyed, even though it hardly captures the composer’s description.

In short, I find Stutzmann’s performance the more compelling of the two. Yet if I want to hear this edition, surely I want Bruckner’s cut in the finale. Rattle’s release adds to the symphony itself the discarded 1874/76 Scherzo and the 1878 Volksfest Finale, an extended early version of the Andante, and the unabridged 1881 Finale, a valuable addition for Bruckner specialists.

Ultimately, neither recording compels me to embrace this version over the more familiar Haas and Nowak editions, and neither matches the brilliance of Blomstedt, Honeck, or the classic Böhm/Vienna/Decca.

Recommended Comparisons
Rattle | Blomstedt | Honeck | Böhm

Review – Piano Postcards – Stephen Hough
Azusa Ueno - July 15, 2026

Sir Stephen Hough’s latest album, Piano Postcards, draws on a wide variety of musical genres. The program blends highlights from Disney films, classical piano repertoire, and international folk music. Many of the selections are Hough’s own transcriptions, continuing the pianist-composer tradition he has championed on previous releases.


Piano Postcards
Amazon
Presto
Apple
The album opens with a three-movement suite from Mary Poppins. Hough’s reading of Chim chim cher-ee (track 1) brings a wistful nostalgia to the jaunty original. His technical finesse is evident throughout, the passagework clear and lithe.

Feed the birds (track 2) captures a deep warmth, drawn from his careful control of the instrument’s middle register. The finale (track 3) closes the suite with energy and unfailing playfulness. Hough approaches the three songs much like a classical sonata, giving the set structural unity while letting each stand on its own.

Moving to a different corner of the world, Hough turns to Taiwanese Hokkien and Japanese traditions, shedding welcome light on non-Western melodies. His Spring breeze prelude (track 8), by Hakka composer Deng Yu-Hsien, brings out the beauty of the original tune in a clever arrangement. Its opening bars quote Rachmaninoff’s Prelude in G major, Op. 32 No. 5, establishing a flow with the Russian repertoire heard earlier. The harmonies are colorful and lush, though the accompaniment occasionally feels too pervasive, obscuring the delicate main melody.

Aka tombo (track 9), a widely recognized Japanese song, receives a transcription of real depth. Hough’s melodic playing captures the natural beauty of the original.

Hough includes further Disney arrangements later in the program. His Reflection from Mulan (track 11) is a high point, turning an already famous song into something richer and more complex. He threads the theme through several voices against lush textures, giving it a neo-Romantic cast that carries his virtuosity lightly.

The arrangement of Do you want to build a snowman? from Frozen (track 10) is similarly intelligent. Hough builds his transcription on the song’s opening motif, and the delicate filigree accompaniment serves a programmatic purpose too, depicting falling snow.

Stephen Hough at the piano in concert
Stephen Hough. Photo © Robert Torres.

The more conventional repertoire is just as strong. Rachmaninoff’s Prelude in G sharp minor (track 5) shows exquisite voice-leading and a sparkle in the accompaniment. In The flight of the bumblebee (track 7), the passagework is a delight, though the interpretation leans toward a surprisingly gentle character rather than an aggressive one.

The lesser-known gems are especially rewarding, particularly the works of Cécile Chaminade. Her Toccata (track 16) is a showpiece that keeps a delicate, meticulous edge even at its most spirited. Hough holds these contrasts in balance, and the reading is both exciting and refined.

Les sylvains (track 18) sets soothing, idyllic outer sections against a lively central episode. Hough moves between the two moods so that the whimsical middle feels like a natural outgrowth rather than a break.

Piano Postcards is not the first thematic curation of eclectic miniatures, but it is one of the most thoughtful. No work or genre feels chosen at random to make the program accessible; instead, a narrative emerges through small details and musical quotations. Together, the transcriptions and the performances confirm Hough’s standing among the finest of today’s pianist-composers.

Review: Orff – Carmina Burana – Detroit Symphony Orchestra, Jader Bignamini
David A. McConnell - July 13, 2026

Let me say straight away that this is a very good performance of Carmina Burana. The choral singing is excellent, the singers balanced well forward so we hear them at every dynamic level. The soloists sing well, each having clearly thought about the text and its characterization. The orchestra plays with energy and exactitude. The wide, deep soundstage lets us hear its tummy-rumbling power at climaxes, but there are quieter moments where the ensemble and solo playing is finely turned. The different components come together in the second part (In Taberna) and through much of the third (Cour d’amours). Conductor Jader Bignamini has a sharp vision of how this music should sound, and has convinced his performers to realize it.


Carmina Burana
Amazon
Presto
Apple
But his interpretation too often strays from what Orff intends. Some tempos are slower than they should be, but more troubling is Bignamini’s unwillingness to generate enough forward momentum. Readers might argue that this is an issue of personal preference, and it is true that all this listener’s favorite performances of Carmina take under an hour (Bignamini takes just under 61 minutes). But there is a reference recording, endorsed by the composer, which should perhaps serve as a guide for how this music is meant to be performed.

Orff was present for Eugen Jochum’s 1967 DG recording with the Chorus and Orchestra of the Deutsche Oper Berlin and three stellar soloists. It has remained in the catalogue ever since, rightly hailed as a classic. That is, at least in part, because Orff was thrilled by the drive and rhythmic verve of Jochum’s interpretation, which takes less than 56 minutes. Jochum highlights the music’s primitivism, the purposeful stripping away of sophistication in favor of raw, elemental force. Orff’s incessant ostinatos and percussive orchestration create earthy, visceral energy, and Jochum understands that, even if the 1960s sound cannot capture it all as well as a digital recording.

Bignamini, on the other hand, often seeks to add sophistication back into the score, to make it more profound than Orff intends. Compare his opening chorus with performances by Ozawa and Rattle (Philips/Universal and Warner Classics). Neither conductor is significantly faster than Bignamini, but both push forward in a way that creates a frenetic atmosphere that makes the Detroit performance seem cautious.

Bignamini’s second movement is not significantly slower than Ozawa, Rattle, Blomstedt (Decca) or Muti (Warner Classics), but his more legato phrasing and articulation weigh the music down. And in the sixth-movement orchestral dance, he suddenly slows for the flute and timpani duet — why? The flute playing is beautiful, but are beauty and shapeliness what the composer wanted? Again, it strikes me as adding sophistication to music that is expressly meant to reject that quality.

The Detroit Symphony Orchestra in concert
The Detroit Symphony Orchestra. Photo © Sarah Smarch.

The use of a countertenor (Reginald Mobley) instead of a tenor also goes against the score. The tessitura of “Olim lacus colueram” is infamously high for a reason — the swan is lamenting that it is being roasted to death over a fire. Most tenors sound uncomfortable singing it, which is exactly what Orff wanted. Mobley, by contrast, sounds too beautiful. Baritone Andrzej Filończyk offers vivid characterization, painted in bold colors, but Fischer-Dieskau (Jochum), Hampson (Ozawa) and Gerhaher (Rattle), all of them excellent Lieder singers, find greater textual expression. And in the final movements (tracks 21–23), soprano Chen Reiss lacks the vocal mastery that makes Gundula Janowitz (Jochum) and, most especially, Arleen Auger (Muti) so ravishing.

The strongest part of the performance is the choral and orchestral work, though I cannot say it betters what we hear from San Francisco (Blomstedt), London (Muti), or Berlin (Ozawa and Rattle). Pentatone’s engineering is truthful and makes no effort to turn the production into a ‘sonic blockbuster.’ First-rate liner notes often point the reader toward the text, yet this booklet offers neither text nor translation. In an already crowded field, this very good performance does not challenge existing recommendations.

Recommended Comparisons
Orff: Carmina Burana
Jochum | Ozawa | Rattle | Blomstedt
