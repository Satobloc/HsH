Overview (intuitively)

You’re coupling four things:
1. the filament/helix (elastic slender body, possibly actively rotating/winding),
2. the surrounding viscous fluid (generates drag on the helix and transmits tractions to the membrane),
3. the membrane surface (surface tension, bending resistance, possible surface viscosity), and
4. contact/interaction laws when the filament touches or penetrates the membrane (normal contact forces, friction, adhesion).

The full physics is: momentum balance for the 3D fluid (Navier–Stokes or Stokes), traction continuity at fluid–membrane and fluid–filament interfaces, membrane force balance (surface stresses from tension + bending + viscous surface stresses) and filament force balance (elastic internal stresses + external fluid + contact). Below: equations.

Reduced (practical) model — useful when you want to simulate without full 3D fluid solves

If the helix is slender and the membrane deforms with small slopes, you can greatly simplify:
1. Replace full fluid with local resistive forces on filament (RFT / slender-body theory):
f_{\text{visc}}(s,t) = -\xi_\parallel (v\cdot t)\,t - \xi_\perp [v - (v\cdot t) t].
2. Model membrane as a 2D elastic sheet with small normal displacement w(x,y,t) (Monge patch). Linearized PDE with surface tension \gamma, bending \kappa, and surface viscous damping c_s:
\rho_s \,\partial_{tt} w + c_s \,\partial_t w = -\gamma \Delta w + \kappa\,\Delta^2 w + f_{\perp}(x,y,t),
where f_\perp is the normal force per unit area applied by the helix (project filament contact forces onto membrane grid).
3. Filament point equation (overdamped / quasi-static):
0 = \partial_s F_{\text{int}} + f_{\text{visc}}(s,t) + f_{\text{contact}}(s,t).
Contact to the membrane provides f_{\text{contact}}(s,t) = -k_n [n\cdot(r - X_{\text{mem}})] n - c_n (v_{\text{rel}}\cdot n)n + \text{tangential friction}, activated when distance < threshold.

This reduced model yields a coupled ODE/PDE system on a 1D filament + 2D membrane, but avoids solving 3D Stokes flow.

⸻

Modeling choices, nondimensional groups & regimes
• Reynolds number Re = \rho_f U L/\mu_f: if Re \ll 1 use Stokes (linear), otherwise use Navier–Stokes.
• Capillary number Ca = \mu_f U/\gamma: ratio of viscous to surface tension effects on membrane.
• Bending number B = \kappa/( \gamma L^2 ): relative importance of bending vs tension.
• Slenderness parameter \epsilon = a/L for filament (radius a, length L): if \epsilon \ll 1, RFT or slender-body theory is appropriate.
• Adhesion/contact stiffness k_n should be large enough to prevent unphysical penetration but not so large as to make time stepping impossible — use implicit integrator or penalty-projection.

⸻

Numerical methods & practical tips
• High-fidelity: Immersed boundary (IB) method, Arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) FEM, or boundary-element method (BEM) + shell FEM for membrane. IB is popular for elastic membranes + filaments in viscous fluid. BEM is efficient for Stokes flow but needs membrane discretization and contact handling.
• Reduced approach: discretize filament (rod elements) and membrane (triangular mesh) and couple with RFT & nodal forces; update membrane PDE with implicit time-stepping for stiffness (bending term), use explicit/IMEX for drag.
• Use implicit time stepping for stiff bending/contact; explicit for purely viscous drag terms (with small time-step).
• For contact, prefer a constraint-based method (Lagrange multipliers) or a penalty + smoothing approach to avoid jitter.
• If you include high-frequency parasitic waves, ensure temporal sampling meets Nyquist for the highest frequency — adaptive timestepping helps.
• Validate in limits: compare to pure RFT predictions or to analytical small-deflection membrane solutions.

⸻

A simple analytical approximation for intuition

If membrane deflections are small and you approximate filament as a line of moving point forces F_i(t) applied to a tension-dominated membrane, the steady displacement due to a localized normal force F at the origin is (Fourier / Greens function solution):
\hat w(\mathbf{k}) = \frac{\hat f(\mathbf{k})}{\gamma |\mathbf{k}|^2 + \kappa |\mathbf{k}|^4},
so long-wave (low-|k|) response dominated by tension, short-wave by bending. This gives a closed-form way to estimate how a single filament contact causes local membrane indentation and the feedback force.

Example parameter recommendations (biological/microscale style)
• fluid viscosity: \mu_f = 10^{-3} Pa·s (water) or larger for viscous skin;
• filament radius a ≪ length L; use \xi_\perp \approx 4\pi\mu_f/(\ln(0.18/\epsilon)), \xi_\parallel \approx 2\pi\mu_f/(\ln(0.18/\epsilon)) (RFT rough estimates);
• membrane tension γ ~ 10^{-5}–10^{-2} N/m (varies widely); bending modulus κ ~ 10^{-19}–10^{-17} J for lipid membranes; for viscous skins increase κ and include c_s.

\rho_f A\,\partial_{tt}\mathbf{r} + C_{\rm int}\partial_t\mathbf{r} = \partial_s\mathbf{F}{\rm int} + \mathbf{f}{\rm fluid} + \mathbf{f}{\rm contact},
with \mathbf{F}{\rm int} from bending/twist constitutive laws (e.g. bending modulus B, twist modulus C).

Fluid coupling (choose level):
• High-fidelity: incompressible Stokes / Navier–Stokes with traction continuity at boundaries.
• Reduced (practical): resistive-force / slender-body drag per unit length
\boxed{\mathbf{f}{\rm fluid}(s,t)\approx -\xi\parallel (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t} - \xi_\perp\big(\mathbf{v}-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{t})\mathbf{t}\big),}
where \mathbf{v}=\partial_t\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{u}_{\infty} and \mathbf{t}=\partial_s\mathbf{r}\,/\,\|\partial_s\mathbf{r}\|.

Membrane (Monge / small-deflection linear model):
Let membrane vertical deflection be w(x,y,t) over a reference plane. Then
\rho_s\partial_{tt}w + c_s\partial_t w = -\gamma\Delta w + \kappa\Delta^2 w + f_\perp(x,y,t),
where f_\perp is the normal pressure/traction from filament contact / fluid.

Contact law (penalty + dashpot + friction):
Activate when signed distance d(s,t)\le d_c:
\mathbf{f}{\rm contact} = H(d_c-d)\Big[-k_n(d_c-d)\mathbf{n}-c_n(\mathbf{v}{\rm rel}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}\Big] + \text{tangential friction}.
(Or enforce nonpenetration with Lagrange multipliers for stability.)

This is the coupled system: geometry → filament equations → fluid drag or full fluid solves → membrane PDE ← contact forces ← filament positions. Solve simultaneously (time-stepping, implicit/explicit mix).

2) What “complete” means here — assumptions & limitations

You should be explicit about which of these you want, because they change complexity enormously.
1. Closure choice (fluid): full Navier–Stokes / Stokes + membrane traction is physically “complete” but expensive. RFT/SBT is cheaper but approximate (valid when filament slenderness ε≪1 and flow is locally quasi-steady).
2. Membrane model: the linear Monge patch is fine for small deflections and moderate curvatures. For large deformations, use a nonlinear shell/Helfrich model with geometric exactness.
3. Contact enforcement: penalty methods are easy but require stiff timesteps; constraint/Lagrange multiplier methods are more robust but more complex.
4. Frame choice & parametrization: use arc-length s for the filament to separate geometry and dynamics. Use a Bishop (parallel transport) frame to avoid artificial twisting.
5. Numerical stiffness: bending, contact, and high-frequency parasitics create stiff dynamics — expect to use implicit (or semi-implicit) timestepping for stable integration.
6. Scale separation: if \Omega\ll\omega_s\ll\omega_p, sampling needs to resolve \omega_p — expensive. You may be able to homogenize or average parasitic effects if you care only about slow behavior.
7. Thermal/noise / stochastic effects: not included; add if microscale Brownian motion matters.
8. Validation: no single formula guarantees accuracy — validate limiting cases (pure helix, pure membrane indentation, no-contact RFT) analytically or against simpler simulations.

Physical picture / interaction channels
1. Direct mechanical contact: when filaments touch — normal repulsion/penalty, tangential friction, adhesion.
2. Hydrodynamic coupling: forces exerted by filament A create fluid flows that move filament B (Stokes / Oseen / boundary-integral coupling). Nonlocal and long-range.
3. Membrane-mediated coupling: filaments deform a shared membrane; those deformations produce forces back on the other filament (Green’s-function convolution).

Key mathematical elements explained (so you can implement them)

Hydrodynamic kernel (continuous)
For Stokes flow the interaction is linear: the velocity at x due to a force density f(y) is
u_i(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\int G_{ij}(x,y)\,f_j(y)\,dy,\qquad
G_{ij}(x,y)=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{|x-y|}+\frac{(x_i-y_i)(x_j-y_j)}{|x-y|^3}.
When you evaluate x on a filament centerline you must regularize the integral (slender-body theory or regularized stokeslets).

Slender-body approximation (SBT)
For a slender filament of small radius a, one often uses
\partial_t r(s) \approx \frac{1}{8\pi\mu}\Big[ \left(-\ln\epsilon\right) \, \big( I + t(s)t(s)\big)\, f(s) + \int_{\text{others}} \big(G(r(s),r(s’)) - \text{local asymptotic}\big) f(s’) ds’ \Big],
with ε = a/L. This gives self-term (local anisotropic drag) plus nonlocal integrals coupling filaments.

Discrete mobility (RPY matrix for beads)
If you discretize filaments into N spherical elements, the Rotne–Prager–Yamakawa tensor gives pairwise mobility M_ij between node i and j:
M_{ij} = \begin{cases}
\frac{1}{6\pi\mu a} I, & i=j,\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{8\pi\mu r_{ij}}\Big[\Big(1+\frac{2a^2}{3r_{ij}^2}\Big)I +\Big(1-\frac{2a^2}{r_{ij}^2}\Big)\hat r_{ij}\hat r_{ij}\Big], & r_{ij}\ge 2a,\\[6pt]
\text{(regularized form)}, & r_{ij}<2a.
\end{cases}
Then V = M F couples nodes across filaments automatically; positive-definite M is numerically stable.

Membrane coupling (linear response)
If the membrane is well-approximated as linear tension+bending, the normal deflection due to a localized normal load f⊥ has Fourier solution
\hat w(k) = \frac{\hat f_\perp(k)}{\gamma |k|^2 + \kappa |k|^4}.
Compute w in physical space via inverse FFT and obtain membrane tractions at filament nodes.

Contact & lubrication
When two surfaces get very close, hydrodynamic lubrication forces dominate (very large normal resistance); RPY/Oseen miss this. Include short-range lubrication corrections or a contact model (penalty + dashpot). For stiff contact you must treat these implicitly.

⸻

Practical numerical recipe (recommended)
1. Discretize filaments into nodes (sufficient nodes per small-helix turn; more if parasitics high-frequency).
2. Choose hydrodynamic model:
• RPY mobility (beads) if you want pairwise decent accuracy + stability.
• Slender-body discretization if you want continuous-force density.
3. Compute internal elastic forces (rod mechanics) at nodes. Enforce inextensibility with constraints (Lagrange multipliers) or stiff springs.
4. Compute contact forces (smoothed penalty) and friction.
5. Compute membrane forces: either solve linear membrane PDE using FFT (fast) or BEM for nonlinear membranes.
6. Form total nodal forces F and compute velocities V = M·F (or solve fluid PDE if doing full IB).
7. Time-step positions: use semi-implicit or fully implicit integrator for bending/contact (backward Euler or BDF2 for stiff terms).
8. Add lubrication correction between nearly-touching nodes (or resolve with contact model).
9. Validate: single filament in fluid (drag), two parallel filaments (hydrodynamic mobilities), membrane indentation Green’s function.

⸻

Important modeling caveats & tips
• Hydrodynamic coupling is long-range — even distant oscillations can produce measurable motions. Use mobility matrix methods for global coupling.
• Lubrication forces scale like 1/d and produce extreme stiffness when surfaces are very close — treat implicitly or use specialized lubrication models.
• If you only care about slow effects of high-frequency parasitics, consider averaging/homogenization to avoid very small time-steps.
• Ensure the mobility (M) you use is symmetric positive-definite (RPY is) to guarantee physically consistent motion and numerical stability.
• For two filaments near or on a deformable membrane, membrane-mediated coupling often decays algebraically in-plane — compute via FFT-based convolution for speed.

How to build the coupling terms practically
1. Choose modal basis φ_n:
• If membrane patch is periodic, Fourier modes are ideal (FFT for fast convolution).
• If clamped/finite, use eigenmodes of plate/tensioned membrane (precompute with FEM) or use a convenient finite-element basis and reduce.
2. Compute modal coefficients of forcing:
• Regularize filament nodal forces with a smooth kernel δ_ε(x−X_i) (Peskin delta or Gaussian).
• For each mode: F_n(t) = ∑_i (φ_n evaluated at X_i) · (f_node_i) · area_weight_i (or integrate on grid then FFT-project).
3. Reconstruct membrane tractions from modal q_n:
• For linear membrane, traction operator is linear: p_mem(x,t) = L[w] = L[∑ q_n φ_n] = ∑ q_n L[φ_n]. Precompute L[φ_n] to speed sampling at filament nodes.
4. Hydrodynamics:
• If you include full fluid, the membrane motion w(x,t) will also create flow (membrane moves relative to fluid). That can be included either in the mobility operator or as extra forcing in the fluid solve. In reduced models you can ignore this term or approximate it by adding membrane nodal velocities into the mobility solve so the no-slip condition is satisfied.

Numerical algorithm (implementation sketch)
1. Discretize filaments (nodes) and choose membrane modal truncation N (start small, N~10–50).
2. Precompute φ_n(x) and traction operators Lφ_n (sampled on nodes/grid).
3. At each time-step:
a. Given R(t), assemble internal forces F_int and contact forces F_contact.
b. Project filament nodal forces onto membrane modes → compute F_m(t).
c. Advance membrane modal ODEs for q(t) (implicit step advisable due to stiffness).
d. Reconstruct membrane traction field at filament nodes from q(t).
e. Assemble total nodal forces F_total (including membrane reaction).
f. Compute node velocities via mobility V = M · F_total (include membrane velocity if enforcing no-slip).
g. Advance filament nodes R ← R + Δt V (or use implicit integrator for stiff contact/bending).
h. Repeat.

2) Variational principle with nonconservative forces (Lagrange–d’Alembert + constraints)

Stationarity with virtual displacements \delta\mathbf{X} satisfying \delta\mathbf{g}=0 gives:
