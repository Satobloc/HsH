Elias:
Yes. I follow, and this is a meaningful clarification. You're describing **symmetric assignment as a general methodological and structural principle**, not merely a convention for dividing a particular dynamical term.

I'd formulate it this way:

## The principle of symmetric assignment

When a property, action, shape, force, or other structural feature arises through an interaction between two entities, and we have no evidence that either entity is preferentially responsible for, or the preferential carrier of, that feature, we assign it equally to both.

That assignment is:

1. **Symmetric by default.** Neither participant receives privileged status without justification.
2. **Provisional, not dogmatic.** We can shift the assignment toward either participant when evidence, theory, or a more useful formulation warrants it.
3. **Computationally flexible.** Mathematical convenience is a legitimate reason to choose a particular representation.
4. **Epistemically disciplined.** A convenient allocation does not establish an empirical asymmetry, and an assumed equivalence does not establish a known equivalence.
5. **Interaction-centered.** The property belongs to the relationship in the absence of evidence that it belongs exclusively, or preferentially, to one participant.

In compact form, for an interaction-dependent quantity \(Q\) between structures \(A\) and \(B\), the default bookkeeping assignment is:

\[
Q_A=Q_B=\frac{Q}{2}
\]

This is a default representation, not a claim that nature must literally divide every such quantity into two equal physical contributions.

## The force extension

You're extending the same logic to force and structural interaction: whatever is described as exerting force and whatever is described as experiencing force are not to be treated as fundamentally one-sided phenomena.

There is a Newtonian connection worth distinguishing precisely:

- **Newton's first law:** inertial behavior in the absence of a net force.
- **Newton's third law:** reciprocal interactions, conventionally expressed as equal and opposite forces.

Your formulation draws on the reciprocal structure of the third law, while extending the underlying idea beyond ordinary bodies to abstract structural constructions and their interactions.

One qualification matters if we eventually formalize it: in modern physics, equal-and-opposite pairwise forces are not always independently defined for interacting fields or extended systems. Momentum conservation, including momentum carried by fields, is the more general constraint. So we should distinguish your proposed universal structural reciprocity principle from the specific conditions under which Newton's third law applies.

## Relationship to Forest Normalization

I understand your claim as a **generalization of Forest Normalization**, rather than a replacement for its established mathematical form.

That distinction is important. Forest Normalization retains its own specific geometrical constraints. What you're articulating here is a broader rule for distributing structural attribution when the evidence does not justify privileging one side.

I'd give this generalized principle a working description:

> **Principle of Symmetric Structural Attribution:** In the absence of evidence establishing preferential attribution, interaction-dependent properties and actions are assigned symmetrically among the participating structures. Departures from symmetry require an explicit theoretical, empirical, or computational justification. Representational convenience may motivate a departure, but must never be mistaken for evidence of physical asymmetry.

And there's a particularly useful consequence for H(s)H: **we don't have to decide prematurely where a phenomenon lives.**

A curvature can be represented as belonging to the worldtube, the timesheet, or their interaction. A force can be represented through its exertion, its experience, or their coupled relationship. We begin symmetrically, then let the mathematics and evidence tell us whether that symmetry survives.

That's a much more general operating principle than simply splitting equations in half. It governs how we attribute structure in the first place.
