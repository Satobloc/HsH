This bold theory says the Big Bang wasn't our beginning
Story by Dr Alastair Gunn • 1h • 2 min read

What is the ekpyrotic universe theory?

It might sound like something you’d find in a Petri dish, but ekpyrotic cosmology is actually an alternative idea to the standard theory of cosmology, which is known as the Lambda-CDM, or ΛCDM model.

In the ΛCDM model, the remarkable flatness of the Universe requires a period of rapid ‘inflation’, which smooths out some of the early variations in density and temperature.

There’s no direct proof of this inflation, or a likely cause, but it does neatly explain many complicated aspects of the Universe we see today, and is perfectly consistent with current observations.

In ekpyrotic theory, however, there is no beginning of time, and the Big Bang is replaced with a bounce between two ‘branes’ – three-dimensional worlds in a five-dimensional space-time.

The structure of the current Universe is determined before this bounce, so the need for inflation is avoided.

The theory also avoids the inconvenient singularity in standard cosmology, when the Universe was somehow all crushed down to a single point before exploding in the Big Bang.

But the theory isn’t perfect. It seems to generate negative-energy particles, which would destroy the Universe.

There are also other differences in the predictions of ekpyrotic and ΛCDM theories, such as the form of the gravitational wave background (random ripples in space-time potentially caused by the force of the Big Bang), the distribution of galaxies and the existence of so-called ghost particles.

Rating:

False (About this rating?)

Claude’s new quantum circuit design uses 17 times fewer gates than earlier approach

Claude’s new quantum circuit design uses 17 times fewer gates than earlier approach
Anthropic’s Claude Mythos 5 has developed a quantum circuit design that uses 17 times fewer gates than an earlier approach. The result could help engineers run demanding quantum algorithms with fewer computational resources. It also offers a new way to tackle one of quantum computing’s biggest engineering challenges: balancing memory requirements against processing time.

The design emerged from a 53-hour experiment led by researcher Andi Gu through Anthropic’s STEM Fellows Program. Claude developed a reversible arithmetic algorithm for elliptic-curve calculations, with researchers from Harvard University and QuEra Computing collaborating on the work. The findings suggest automated circuit design could accelerate progress toward practical quantum computing.

Rethinking quantum circuit design
Quantum computers process information through qubits, which can represent combinations of states through superposition. However, existing machines have only hundreds to thousands of qubits, and environmental disturbances can introduce errors during computation.

Engineers must therefore make quantum circuits as efficient as possible. Every operation consumes computational resources, while storing intermediate results requires additional qubits. Quantum circuits also cannot simply discard temporary information, since their operations must remain reversible.

Anthropic tasked Claude with optimizing eight computational building blocks across chemistry, physics simulation, optimization, number theory, and numerical computing. An automated evaluator checked each design for correctness and scored its resource requirements.

Claude Mythos 5 worked through repeated design-and-test cycles, refining its proposals without human input between rounds. Its results improved on previously published designs, with some reductions reaching an order of magnitude or more.

Solving a memory problem
One particularly notable result concerns modular inversion, a mathematical operation used in elliptic-curve arithmetic. The operation plays a central role in quantum algorithms targeting the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem.

Claude focused on an algorithm called divsteps, which simplifies numerical values through repeated conditional operations. Classical computers can efficiently execute this method, but translating it into quantum circuits creates a major storage problem.

A reversible circuit must preserve enough information to reconstruct every computational step. Divsteps therefore requires a record of its branching decisions, potentially consuming enormous numbers of additional qubits.

Claude developed a way to recover those decisions directly from intermediate numerical values. It derived mathematical identities to establish the method, tested the approach through numerical simulations, and formalized its correctness using Lean.

The resulting circuit eliminates the need to store the complete history of branching decisions. Claude then explored hybrid designs that store some information and reconstruct the rest.

One design uses 12 percent more qubits than the smallest previous design but requires 17 times fewer gates. That trade-off expands the available options for engineers working with limited quantum hardware.

Shortening quantum computing’s timeline
Anthropic also reported improvements across its other circuit-design tasks. One optimization reduced the costliest step of a leading quantum optimization algorithm by approximately 12 times.

The findings follow years of progress in reducing the resources needed for useful quantum computations. Researchers estimated in 2017 that simulating the ground-state energy of FeMoco, an iron-sulfur cluster involved in biological nitrogen fixation, would require roughly 50 trillion Toffoli gates.

Subsequent advances brought that estimate down to approximately 340 million gates, a reduction of about 150,000 times. Anthropic argues that automated circuit optimization could help accelerate similar progress.

Important obstacles remain. Quantum computers still need better error correction, reliable hardware controls, and systems that can manage fragile qubits during extended calculations. Even so, AI-designed circuits could help researchers use future machines more efficiently.

A highway instead of a racetrack: Strong coupling of light and sound in an optical fiber
Story by Edda Fischer • 13h • 3 min read


Original raw measurement of strong coupling of optical and traveling acoustic waves in an optical fiber. Credit: Laura Blázquez Martínez
Original raw measurement of strong coupling of optical and traveling acoustic waves in an optical fiber. Credit: Laura Blázquez Martínez
For the first time, scientists have achieved strong coupling between light and long-lived acoustic vibrations in an optical fiber. Optical fibers are used in both classical telecommunications and quantum communication and could serve not only as connections between computing nodes but also as quantum processors themselves in the future. The results, published in Nature Physics, pave the way for new technologies in quantum signal processing.

Mechanical and acoustic vibrations can be used to convert signals across various frequency ranges, from optical frequencies (terahertz) to microwaves (gigahertz) and even lower frequencies (megahertz or kilohertz). For instance, this capability is essential to connect multiple quantum computers operating at gigahertz frequencies via optical links. Furthermore, acoustic waves propagate at speeds significantly lower than the speed of light. So, they provide a medium capable of transporting slowly moving information—which is of great interest for data storage and the temporary buffering of information.

However, to utilize one of these processing capabilities for quantum information via mechanical vibrations, one prerequisite is needed: the strong coupling regime. This regime ensures that quantum information is exchanged before it is lost due to losses and decoherence. Decoherence describes the state in which the waves no longer oscillate in unison. In the strong coupling regime, quantum information can be processed more quickly—faster than the rate at which noise enters the system—without losing quantum properties.

Until now, the strong coupling regime has been achieved in so-called optomechanical cavities, which can be understood as racetracks for light. Light speeds around these optical cavity "racetracks" up to a million times, giving it the opportunity to strongly couple with vibrations along the cavity path. However, when light travels through an optical fiber, it is akin to driving on a highway. The optical signals speed in one direction along the "fiber highway" and pass vibrations or other obstacles only once, instead of a million times on a racetrack. Consequently, achieving strong coupling between optical information and an acoustic vibration during a single pass is extremely difficult.

Strong coupling between light and sound achieved for the first time without an optical or mechanical resonator
Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light (MPL) and Leibniz University Hannover (LUH) for the first time have achieved strong coupling within a cooled optical fiber via light-sound interactions—specifically through the effect of stimulated Brillouin scattering. This state of strong coupling between light and sound is realized without an optical resonator or a mechanical resonator. In a single pass of the light through an optical fiber at moderate cryogenic temperatures of 4 K, the team led by Birgit Stiller succeeded in generating light-sound interactions much stronger than the losses caused by both optical and acoustic noise sources.

A gap appears in the spectral signal measurement—which the researchers interpret as evidence of strong coupling. "We have introduced a new measurement technique that allows us to observe the clearest sign of strong coupling: precisely this gap in the spectral response, which is otherwise only visible in resonators," says Laura Blázquez Martínez, first author of the study. The phenomenon of avoided crossing is normally observable in resonator configurations (racetrack), but not in inline operation (highway). Consequently, the new experimental technique represents an important proof of concept of in-fiber strong coupling.

"This result is a crucial step toward utilizing optical fibers as a powerful platform for quantum interactions," says Stiller, head of the Quantum Optoacoustics research group at MPL and professor at the LUH. "The demonstration that this state can be achieved in optical fibers paves the way for performing quantum measurements on the platform that already underpins the bulk of our telecommunications system."

Furthermore, this result has implications for research into the transition between classical and quantum physics. Optical fibers are far more massive objects than the micro-optomechanical resonators typically used to study the interactions between light and mechanical waves. The ability to study the quantum properties of this interaction opens a means of studying the quantum properties of massive objects.

Astronomers find first planet with Earth-like water and weather outside our solar system
Story by Briley Kenney • 12h • 3 min read


Wise 0855 cold brown dwark discovered with concept image from NASA.
Wise 0855 cold brown dwark discovered with concept image from NASA.
© NASA/JPL-Caltech/Penn State University
Through recent observations, astronomers have discovered that an exoplanet — technically a brown dwarf — which we've known about for years, is much more like our home planet Earth than previously believed. The world, designated WISE 0855, has water clouds that are changing thickness, indicating that it also has progressive weather. WISE 0855 is a Jupiter-sized brown dwarf located about 7.5 light years away. Imaged all the way back in 2010 by the Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE), it was officially discovered by Kevin Luhman in 2014 while looking through the data. Initial observations revealed WISE 0855 to appear bluer than it actually is because the imaging was contaminated by background sources. The recent observations revealed much more, including the water and cloud cover patterns.

The University of Arizona's Steward Observatory team, responsible for the newest discoveries, used the James Webb Space Telescope infrared observatory to watch the brown dwarf for 11 hours. With the JWST, the Steward Observatory team noticed clouds coming in and out of focus, changing over time as WISE 0855 rotates — though things work a little differently than the simple weather changes back home since the atmosphere contains gases rising from deep below. Ultimately, WISE 0855 likely isn't a habitable world and is probably much closer in similarity to Jupiter than to Earth. In addition, brown dwarfs like WISE 0855 aren't considered to be true planets.

The first is that WISE 0855 is only 7.5 light years away — about 44 trillion miles — and alongside the fact that it's the coldest brown dwarf ever discovered, it's also relatively close, cosmically speaking. To give a comparison, astronomically, the closest star system to Earth is Proxima Centauri, which is about 4.25 light years away. WISE 0855's distance puts it in just about the same neighborhood — a mere 3.25 light years further than Proxima.

The other notable element is that the James Webb Space Telescope was able to detect the clouds, their movement, and their general composition to allow for true observations. This is the first time a team like this has been able to confirm water clouds and also observe them actually growing thicker and thinning out. From the Inside Flap
COMPREHENSIVE COVERAGE OF NONLINEAR PROGRAMMING THEORY AND ALGORITHMS, THOROUGHLY REVISED AND EXPANDED

Nonlinear Programming: Theory and Algorithmsnow in an extensively updated Third Edition addresses the problem of optimizing an objective function in the presence of equality and inequality constraints. Many realistic problems cannot be adequately represented as a linear program owing to the nature of the nonlinearity of the objective function and/or the nonlinearity of any constraints. The Third Edition begins with a general introduction to nonlinear programming with illustrative examples and guidelines for model construction.

Concentration on the three major parts of nonlinear programming is provided:

Convex analysis with discussion of topological properties of convex sets, separation and support of convex sets, polyhedral sets, extreme points and extreme directions of polyhedral sets, and linear programming
Optimality conditions and duality with coverage of the nature, interpretation, and value of the classical Fritz John (FJ) and the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions; the interrelationships between various proposed constraint qualifications; and Lagrangian duality and saddle point optimality conditions
Algorithms and their convergence, with a presentation of algorithms for solving both unconstrained and constrained nonlinear programming problems
Important features of the Third Edition include:

New topics such as second interior point methods, nonconvex optimization, nondifferentiable optimization, and more
Updated discussion and new applications in each chapter
Detailed numerical examples and graphical illustrations
Essential coverage of modeling and formulating nonlinear programs
Simple numerical problems
Advanced theoretical exercises
The book is a solid reference for professionals as well as a useful text for students in the fields of operations research, management science, industrial engineering, applied mathematics, and also in engineering disciplines that deal with analytical optimization techniques. The logical and self-contained format uniquely covers nonlinear programming techniques with a great depth of information and an abundance of valuable examples and illustrations that showcase the most current advances in nonlinear problems.

CHAPTERS 0:00 Sources of mass 2:33 Particles are excitations in fields 6:09 How mass comes from interaction with Higgs 10:42 Why do some particles interact and others don't? 11:31 How our universe would not exist without Higgs SUMMARY All mass is a form of energy. About 99% of an atom’s mass is contained in the binding energy within the nucleus; roughly 1% is in the masses of the subatomic particles (electrons and quarks). In the Standard Model, every particle is an excitation of its field. The Higgs field has a nonzero vacuum expectation value (~246 GeV); particles that couple to it gain interaction energy with this vacuum value, which appears as rest mass. The coupling strength determines how much mass a particle acquires. Particles that do not couple to the Higgs (for example, photons and gluons) remain massless in the Standard Model. This mechanism, spontaneous symmetry breaking, gives mass to the W and Z bosons, electrons, and quarks. Neutrinos have tiny masses observed experimentally, but their origin is not fully explained within the Standard Model and may involve physics beyond it. Matching impedance for faster charge detection
The study, published in the journal npj 2D Materials and Applications, solves a technical bottleneck in quantum physics: the inability to measure high-resistance 2D-material-based quantum devices at high speeds. The team achieved this by integrating a variable capacitor made from strontium titanate (SrTiO₃) into a high-frequency resonant circuit.

Faster readouts could accelerate qubit development
This innovation solves a long-standing bottleneck in quantum physics. While 2D materials like bilayer graphene hold promise for creating stable qubits with exceptionally long coherence times, the high electrical resistance of quantum devices made from these materials has historically blocked sensitive high-frequency measurements. Without a way to match impedance, fast and accurate state readout was considered difficult.

"Evaluating quantum states quickly is essential, but the high resistance of 2D materials got in the way," Otsuka explains. "Because qubit development requires massive amounts of repetitive statistical measurements, a fast readout technique acts as an essential catalyst. This foundational technology will significantly accelerate the overall research and development cycle of 2D-material-based quantum devices."

Most normal matter in the universe isn't found in planets, stars or galaxies – an astronomer explains where it's distributed
Story by Chris Impey • 9mo • 5 min read

This article was originally published at The Conversation. The publication contributed the article to Space.com's Expert Voices: Op-Ed & Insights.

If you look across space with a telescope, you'll see countless galaxies, most of which host large central black holes, billions of stars and their attendant planets. The universe teems with huge, spectacular objects, and it might seem like these massive objects should hold most of the universe's matter.

But the Big Bang theory predicts that about 5% of the universe's contents should be atoms made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Most of those atoms cannot be found in stars and galaxies – a discrepancy that has puzzled astronomers.

If not in visible stars and galaxies, the most likely hiding place for the matter is in the dark space between galaxies. While space is often referred to as a vacuum, it isn't completely empty. Individual particles and atoms are dispersed throughout the space between stars and galaxies, forming a dark, filamentary network called the "cosmic web."

Throughout my career as an astronomer, I've studied this cosmic web, and I know how difficult it is to account for the matter spread throughout space.

In a study published in June 2025, a team of scientists used a unique radio technique to complete the census of normal matter in the universe.

The census of normal matter
The most obvious place to look for normal matter is in the form of stars. Gravity gathers stars together into galaxies, and astronomers can count galaxies throughout the observable universe.

The census comes to several hundred billion galaxies, each made of several hundred billion stars. The numbers are uncertain because many stars lurk outside of galaxies. That's an estimated 1023 stars in the universe, or hundreds of times more than the number of sand grains on all of Earth's beaches. There are an estimated 1082 atoms in the universe.

Expand article logo  Continue reading

However, this prodigious number falls far short of accounting for all the matter predicted by the Big Bang. Careful accounting indicates that stars contain only 0.5% of the matter in the universe. Ten times more atoms are presumably floating freely in space. Just 0.03% of the matter is elements other than hydrogen and helium, including carbon and all the building blocks of life.

Experiment shows liquid metal TIM doesn't dry out; it turns solid
Story by Shane Downing • 23h • 4 min read


 Experiment shows liquid metal TIM doesn’t dry out; it turns solid  
Experiment shows liquid metal TIM doesn’t dry out; it turns solid
© Thermal Grizzly
YouTuber hardware enthusiast and Thermal Grizzly boss der8auer tested a two-year-old liquid metal thermal interface material (TIM) application with an electron microscope in his new video, discovering that it doesn’t dry out. Today, our society is going through a technological transformation of its own in the form of AI. From advanced AI assistants and agents to companion chatbots and driverless cars, the current revolution differs from any in our lifetime in speed, scope, and scale. In the course of less than five years, we’ve gone from systems that struggled with two-digit multiplication to models that are advancing the frontiers of mathematics, and from AI-generated art that got the number of fingers on a human hand wrong to real-time AI-generated video personas who increasingly can’t be distinguished from the real thing.

The AI revolution has another feature in common with that of literacy, namely that it seems to be changing the way we think. The scientific evidence on this front is fragile and new, and the pace of change means that studies age rapidly. However, early studies suggest a mixed picture, with genuine positives alongside risks. For example, AI can speed up and extend learning, especially when carefully fitted to the task and used with human expertise. One study conducted in Benin City, Nigeria, gave students access to an after-school generative AI tutor with support from their teachers, and over the six weeks of the study they made gains the World Bank assessed as equivalent to nearly two years of typical learning.

However, it’s not quite the free lunch that it appears to be. AI can also foster a kind of cognitive dependence, improving performance when people have access to it, but limiting it when they don’t. One 2023 study assessed learning among Turkish high-school maths students. The control group was given textbooks, but the two experimental groups had either an off-the-shelf version of GPT-4 or a specialised tutor version that gave hints rather than answers. Students using off-the-shelf GPT-4 saw their practice scores rise by some 48 per cent, but in the closed-book exam that followed they scored 17 per cent below the textbook group, suggesting overreliance (notably, the hint-giving tutor did not cause a comparable loss).

It is tempting to file this under moral panic. But there was real merit in Plato’s concerns. To return briefly to Homer, a question that has arisen since the earliest days of his poems’ reception was whether they were the product of a literate or illiterate mind. Many assumed that the complexity and scale of the poems would have been impossible without the aid of writing. A key contribution to this debate came in the 1930s from the American classicist Milman Parry, who travelled to the Balkans to study the living oral tradition of the South Slavic bards.

Technology, in other words, allows us to do more and think in new ways, thereby making old knowledge and skills unnecessary, and thus fostering dependence

Many of these singers were illiterate, but nonetheless had extraordinary memories for their material, able to perform complex poems thousands of lines long without any written aids, composing them anew in each performance, thus bolstering the case that the Iliad and Odyssey might be products of an oral tradition. Of course, part of the reason that these bards were able to perform such feats of memory and improvisation is that their livelihood depended on it. If necessity is the mother of invention, then the removal of that necessity may lead to those same abilities being lost.

Technology, in other words, allows us to do more and think in new ways, thereby making old knowledge and skills unnecessary, and thus fostering dependence. But our species has been making such trades since before we were even Homo sapiens. Fire and cooking allowed our ancestors to draw more energy from less food, and their bodies came to depend on it, with smaller teeth, jaws, and guts. Farming let our ancestors feed far more mouths than foraging ever could, but in the process made deep knowledge of the natural world – knowledge of herbs and plants, and what mushrooms and berries were safe to eat – less necessary.

Reading, in turn, reshaped the brain itself: the neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues found that literacy re-maps part of the visual system for written words, at a small cost to how it responds to faces. As the philosopher Andy Clark has argued, we are “natural-born cyborgs”, our nature defined by its capacity for extension, manifest in the plasticity of our brains and the flexibility of our culture.

No other species on Earth remakes itself on anything like the scale humans do. Squirrels, bears, and pangolins may adapt their behaviours to shifting ecological conditions (typically those imposed by human activity), but their natures are made of stone, not clay, weathered by the slow winds and waves of Darwinian evolution.

Insofar as there are any non-human exceptions to this rule, it is the animals we have brought with us: cattle, sheep, pigs, goats, cats, and above all, dogs and horses. While it is common these days to characterise domestication as a two-way process – dogs, as they say, partially domesticated themselves – it was life alongside humans, and human choices, that remade them.

The synthetic forms of intelligence we are now moulding are in a sense a continuation of that process. They may be at once stranger and yet somehow more human than those of animals, but they are far from our first fellow travellers. This capacity and drive to shape and reshape both ourselves and the world around us is a trait that has been with us since long before Homer’s time. His world is already one full of innovations – a world of ships and armies, spears and shields, horses and chariots, nations warring and trading, kings and queens, temples and cities and farms. Change may be core to our nature, but we are not helpless before it.

The Turkish classroom study showed that a tool that handed over answers produced quite different results from one that merely beckoned students towards truth. As we enter what may be the most ambitious chapter in our species’ history, it remains to be seen what benefits and losses machine intelligence will bring. But we should be clear-eyed and see the process for what it is – the next step in the strange journey of a strange species, Homo mutabilis, whose essence is change.

Scientists may have found why humans gained consciousness

Scientists may have found why humans gained consciousness
Scientists may have found why humans gained consciousness
Human consciousness once looked like an evolutionary extravagance, a mysterious inner movie with no clear purpose. Now a wave of research is converging on a more grounded answer, suggesting that awareness emerged as a practical tool for survival, social coordination, and flexible decision making rather than a cosmic accident. Scientists are not only tracing how this capacity arose in the brain, they are also beginning to explain why humans, in particular, pushed it to such elaborate heights.

Instead of treating consciousness as an all-or-nothing gift that appeared suddenly in our species, new work frames it as a layered adaptation that built on older neural systems shared with other animals. From the stochastic firing of early nervous systems to the intricate neocortex of modern humans, the story that is taking shape is less about a single breakthrough and more about a long evolutionary climb toward self-reflection, planning, and culture.

The evolutionary puzzle of consciousness
When I look at the current science, the central question is no longer whether consciousness has an evolutionary role, but what that role actually is. One influential line of work argues that awareness evolved to help organisms model themselves and others, turning raw sensation into a flexible guide for behavior in complex environments. In this view, recognizing oneself in a mirror is not a philosophical parlor trick, it is a concrete example of reflexive consciousness that allows an individual to track its own body and intentions, a capacity that Nov researchers link to the ability to navigate society and coordinate with others.

By treating consciousness as a property of advanced nervous systems that was shaped by selection pressures, rather than as a mysterious add-on, scientists can start to connect the dots between individual introspection and the broader demands of group living.

Consciousness as a product of advanced nervous systems
From an evolutionary standpoint, I find it most convincing to treat consciousness as something that rides on the architecture of the nervous system rather than floating above it. One detailed theoretical account argues that consciousness is a property of advanced nervous systems and therefore a direct product of evolution, not a separate substance or force. Seen through that lens, the brain becomes a kind of biological prediction engine that uses conscious states to integrate sensory inputs, memories, and goals into a coherent field of experience. This is not a vague metaphor; it is a claim that particular configurations of neurons, synapses, and feedback loops generate the subjective feel of seeing red or deciding to move a hand. By tying awareness to the physical details of advanced nervous systems, researchers can compare species, developmental stages, and even clinical conditions to map which features of the brain are necessary for conscious processing and which are merely supportive.

From stochastic neurons to subjective experience
One of the more intriguing threads in the current literature is the idea that randomness inside the brain is not just noise, but a key ingredient in how consciousness emerged. Early nervous systems were not perfectly deterministic machines; they were shaped by what one study calls the Highlights of neural evolution, including the Stochasticity of neural system activity. That stochasticity, or built-in variability, may have allowed organisms to explore different behavioral options, making their responses less predictable to predators and competitors and opening the door to more flexible forms of decision making.

As I read it, this perspective suggests that subjective experience grew out of systems that had to manage and harness that internal variability rather than eliminate it. When neural networks became complex enough, the brain needed a way to integrate competing signals, weigh uncertain outcomes, and maintain a stable sense of self across fluctuating internal states. Conscious awareness, on this account, is the brain’s solution to its own complexity: a higher level process that monitors, selects, and stabilizes patterns of activity that would otherwise remain chaotic. The integration of objective measures of neural firing with subjective reports of experience in this research helps bridge the gap between the physical and the felt.

The neocortex and the human upgrade
To understand why human consciousness looks so different from that of other animals, I keep coming back to the structure of the human brain, especially the neocortex. Clinical and experimental work has shown that the emergence of consciousness, as judged by the return of a response to command in patients, is correlated primarily with activity in specific brain regions rather than with a vague global awakening. One influential account describes a neocortical view in which awareness depends on an intricately folded neocortex that supports high level integration of sensory, motor, and cognitive functions, a relationship detailed in research on the neocortical view of consciousness and its link to an intricately folded neocortex.

That anatomical emphasis helps explain why humans, with their expanded neocortex, can sustain complex inner narratives, long term planning, and abstract reasoning that go far beyond immediate sensory demands. The same basic neural machinery that supports a simple response to command in a recovering patient scales up, in our species, into the capacity to imagine future selves, rehearse social interactions, and construct moral frameworks. In evolutionary terms, the neocortex looks less like a luxury and more like a platform that allowed consciousness to become a powerful general purpose tool for modeling the world and our place in it.

Competing theories, converging questions
Even as the evidence for an evolutionary role of consciousness grows, the field is far from unified on how exactly awareness is implemented in the brain. A recent overview of the science lays out a set of major theories, from global workspace models that emphasize broadcasting information across the brain to frameworks that focus on integrated information or recurrent processing. The authors argue that a selection of these theories provides a useful lens for tracking progress, but they also stress that the next phase of research will need to move beyond verbal debates and toward precise, testable predictions about which neural signatures truly mark conscious processing, a challenge summarized in a broad survey of consciousness science, where we are, where we are going, and how theories guide experiments.

From my perspective, what matters for the evolutionary story is that these theories increasingly treat consciousness as graded and multi layered rather than as a single switch that flips on in humans alone. That shift aligns with comparative work across species and with developmental data in humans, both of which suggest that different aspects of awareness, such as basic sensory experience, attention, and self reflection, can come apart and evolve at different rates. The theoretical diversity is not a sign of failure so much as a reflection of how many levels of organization, from molecules to social behavior, have to be integrated to explain why consciousness is useful enough to be favored by natural selection.

Multiple levels of awareness in evolving brains
One way I find helpful to organize this complexity is to think in terms of levels of consciousness that build on each other. Comparative studies of animals propose that there are at least three such levels, each serving a distinct evolutionary function. At the most basic level, organisms maintain simple alertness and responsiveness to the environment; at a higher level, they can integrate sensory inputs into a unified scene; and at the most advanced level, they can reflect on their own mental states and those of others. This tiered view is supported by work showing that Three Levels of Consciousness Serve different evolutionary purposes and support increasingly sophisticated interactions with the world.

Humans appear to have elaborated the top tier, turning self reflection into a platform for culture, technology, and law. Yet the lower levels remain essential; without basic alertness and integrated perception, our higher order reasoning would have nothing to work with. By mapping these levels onto specific neural systems and behavioral capacities, researchers can ask more precise questions about when in evolution each layer emerged and why some lineages, such as primates and certain birds, seem to have climbed higher up the ladder than others.

Birds, mirrors, and the non mammalian route to awareness
Perhaps the most striking challenge to a human centered view of consciousness comes from birds. Despite having brains that look very different from mammalian neocortex, some bird species show behavioral and neural signatures that suggest high levels of awareness. In one set of studies, scientists analyzed avian brain activity and found that birds can achieve complex forms of consciousness without the exact cortical structures found in humans, indicating that evolution can arrive at similar functional outcomes through different anatomical routes, a point highlighted in work showing that the second study analyzed consciousness in birds and found that high levels of consciousness can be achieved in animals.

These findings dovetail with broader comparative research that asks why some species evolved consciousness while others remained relatively unaware. One line of evidence suggests that ecological demands and social complexity play a decisive role: species that need to navigate intricate social hierarchies, long distance migrations, or tool use may benefit from richer internal models of the world. In that context, it is telling that Scientists studying animal consciousness report that Birds achieved awareness without a mammalian style neocortex, suggesting that the evolutionary “why” of consciousness is more about function than about any one brain blueprint.

Consciousness before humans and beneath the cortex
If birds and other animals can be conscious in their own ways, then human awareness is clearly not the starting point of the story. Recent work on brain activity in non human species and in subcortical regions of the human brain suggests that consciousness may have begun long before humans evolved and may not depend exclusively on the outer cortical layers. Experiments that disrupt or stimulate deep brain structures show what one report calls Shocking effects beneath the cortex that indicate subcortical regions play a role in shaping awareness, challenging the idea that the neocortex alone is the seat of consciousness.

For me, this line of evidence reinforces the view that consciousness is layered on top of older survival circuits rather than replacing them. Basic affective states, such as fear or pleasure, and fundamental drives, such as hunger, are rooted in ancient brain regions that long predate humans. As cortical systems expanded, they appear to have integrated and refined these older signals, turning raw urges into nuanced feelings and deliberate choices. The evolutionary “why” of human consciousness, in this light, is not that awareness suddenly appeared in our lineage, but that our brains learned to orchestrate and interpret a rich symphony of signals that had been playing for hundreds of millions of years.

Bridging grand theories with evolutionary detail
Not all explanations of consciousness stay close to the biology. Some theorists have proposed that awareness is a fundamental feature of the universe, perhaps even emerging at the Big Bang, and that brains merely tap into or organize this underlying field. A survey of such ideas notes that There are many trials by contemporary scientists and philosophers to explain the genesis of consciousness, including proposals by figures such as Roger Penrose, and it catalogs how these speculative frameworks attempt to redefine what consciousness is and where it comes from, as outlined in a review that emphasizes that There are many trials by contemporary scientists and philosophers to explain the genesis of consciousness.

While these cosmological accounts are provocative, the evolutionary data increasingly favor models that tie consciousness to the specific history of nervous systems on Earth. That does not rule out deeper metaphysical questions, but it does mean that any satisfying theory has to explain why awareness tracks the development of brains, why it scales with neural complexity, and why it appears to confer concrete survival advantages. In my view, the most promising work is the kind that connects high level concepts, such as integrated information or global broadcasting, with the gritty details of how neurons fire, how animals behave, and how capacities like self recognition in mirrors emerge in Children as their brains mature.

Layered minds and the human edge
When I step back from the individual studies, a coherent picture starts to emerge. Consciousness looks less like a single trait and more like a stack of abilities: basic wakefulness, integrated perception, flexible attention, self monitoring, and social mind reading. Each layer appears to have its own evolutionary logic, from the need to stay alive in a changing environment to the need to predict the behavior of allies and rivals. Humans did not invent these layers from scratch; we inherited and elaborated them, turning a survival toolkit into the foundation for art, science, and politics.

That layered view also helps explain why consciousness can fail in such specific ways, as in anesthesia, coma, or certain neurological disorders, and why different species show different mixes of capacities. It suggests that the reason humans gained such elaborate inner lives is that our ancestors lived in worlds where modeling themselves and others, coordinating in groups, and planning far into the future paid off in reproductive success. The science is still far from complete, but the direction is clear: by treating consciousness as an evolved property of advanced, stochastic, and socially embedded nervous systems, I can finally see a path toward explaining not just how we experience the world, but why that experience exists at all.

While the graphics did purport to show that the price of some household goods and utilities rose less during the second Trump administration than during Biden's presidential term, its figures appeared to be false (wrong), fake (fabricated) or both, and the comparison it made wasn't accurate or fair.

Expand article logo  Continue reading

We could not replicate any of the rate-of-inflation changes the graphic indicated took place between January 2021 and January 2025 or between January 2025 and August 2026 using the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) database. Importantly, it's the same database the graphic said it pulled its data from.

The graphic also made an unfair comparison by directly comparing Biden's 48-month presidency to the 20 months between January 2025 and August 2026 (both included) of Trump's second term. Because inflation is measured over time, direct comparisons should use time periods of the same length. A "fair" comparison in this case might have compared either the first 20 months of Biden's presidency to the first 20 months of Trump's or Biden's full term to Trump's full first term.
