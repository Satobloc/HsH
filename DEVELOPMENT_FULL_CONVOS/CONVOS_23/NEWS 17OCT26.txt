Astronomers just found the first green star. Here’s why it matters
Story by Andrew Griffin • 1d • 3 min read

Astronomers have found the first ever green star, in a study that shows white dwarves can actually come in a wide array of different colours.

The colour of most stars is decided by their colour: when they are cooler, they are orange, whereas our own warmer Sun is white, and the very hottest are a pale blue. Previously, researchers had thought that white dwarves – the remains that are left behind after stars like our Sun die, would work in the same way.

But new research suggests that, despite their name, white dwarves can actually come in a wide array of colours. If we were able to get up close with them, they would come in pink, cyan, yellow, purple – and even green, according to the authors of the study, who found the first such example ever seen.

“Most stars, including most white dwarfs, behave like ‘perfect radiators,’ so their colour is set almost entirely by temperature. But nobody had really looked into what else might be possible for white dwarfs,” said Mark Hollands, from the University of Warwick, who led the work. “It turns out these incredibly dense stars can produce colours nobody expected, and some of them genuinely surprised me."

White dwarves are unusual because they are so dense, the researchers say: they are millions of times denser than the outer shell of the Sun, roughly the same as water. That density means that any elements that might be around are able to interact with the plasma in the white dwarf, absorbing light across many more colours.

As such, those white dwarves are not those perfect radiators. Instead, they will reflect the chemical makeup of the material that are around them.

There is not a white dwarf near enough for us to see its colour. Instead, researchers in the new study calculated it, using data about its spectrum, the “fingerprint” that can be found by splitting starlight apart into its different wavelengths.

Scientists were then able to run that data through a model that simulates how the light would look to a human who was looking at them. As such, they were able to calculate the stars’ true colour.

It showed that the colour is decided by the material in the star’s atmosphere, putting them in different groups. Those with lots of carbon will appear pink or purple, others that are surrounded by pieces of old planetary systems will look yellow, and cooler ones with hydrogen and helium atmospheres will be blue.

But researchers were most excited by WD J1330+6435, a white dwarf that is about 4,000 degrees Celsius and has taken on rocky material from the planetary system for which it was once the Sun. That material has added calcium, lithium, and sodium to the atmosphere, making it glow with a chartreuse green.

It is the first star of that colour ever found, the researchers say.

“The traces of sodium in the atmosphere of this white dwarf absorb the orange part of the spectrum, leaving behind a ghostly green glow,” said Dr Hollands.

“Imagine standing on a planet and looking up at a green star. It feels like something out of science fiction."

They also found what they believe to be the reddest star ever seen. WD J1011+2845 is relatively nearby – just 48 light years away – and carbon is being lifted up to its surface which then distorts the light so that almost all of the light is squeezed into particular wavelengths that make it glow a deep salmon-pink.

Most of the white dwarves that are nearby are still white. Of the 151 examples in the study, which are all within about 65 light years of Earth, five of them were unusually coloured, with two yellow and three pink.

The remaining 146 averaged out to be a cool white. But even that helps adjust our understanding of their colour: astronomers previously believed that white dwarves are generally blue, but that is because those examples are brighter and so can be seen from further away.

The work is reported in a new paper, ‘Any colour you like: The diverse chromaticities of white dwarfs’, published in the journal RAS Techniques and Instruments.

Claim:

New York Democrats are fining unvaccinated Amish children $2,000 per day, as social media posts claimed in October 2026.

Rating:

Mixture (About this rating?)

What's True:

In 2022, New York fined three Amish schools for not complying with a law mandating schools only accept students with updated vaccines, following the 2019 removal of a religious exemption from that law. The small schools were fined $2,000 for every student without updated vaccines every day, for a total of $118,000 before the schools sued the state.

 
What's False:

New York fined the schools, not the children.

 
What's Undetermined:

The New York Department of Health did not clarify to Snopes whether it had fined any other schools or whether it had continued to fine the three schools since 2022.

 
In early October, some social media posts claimed the state of New York had "declared war on the Amish" with vaccine requirements enforced by fines. Many Amish communities have lower childhood vaccination rates than the rest of the U.S. population, although vaccination is not universally rejected in these communities.

For example, one Facebook post (archived) claimed the state was fining each unvaccinated Amish child $2,000 per day. The post asserted that New York Democrats took away the religious exemption often used by Amish families to opt out of childhood vaccine requirements.


Related Facebook post

Shared from Facebook

Users shared similar posts claiming New York Democrats were fining unvaccinated Amish children $2,000 each day on Instagram (archived) and X (archived). Snopes readers emailed us asking whether the rumor was true.

It's true that New York fined at least three Amish schools $2,000 for each unvaccinated child it allowed to attend the school each day. New York did not fine the children themselves, and it's unclear whether the state is still actively imposing fines on these schools or any other over their vaccination policies. Therefore, we've rated this claim a mixture of true and false information.

These details are outlined in a lawsuit, Miller v. McDonald. 

According to the case, in 2019 New York repealed the religious exemption to its school immunization law — which applies to all public, private and religious schools in the state — after a measles outbreak in 2018 and 2019 centered around communities with clusters of unvaccinated people (PDF Page 3).

Democrats were the majority in both the state Senate and state Assembly in 2019. The governor at the time was Andrew Cuomo, a Democrat.

Silicon qubits surpass 99.99% gate fidelity while operating three at once
Story by Science X staff • 1d • 3 min read


Rendered illustration of five silicon spin qubits controlled in parallel through a shared microwave line. Credit: RIKEN
Rendered illustration of five silicon spin qubits controlled in parallel through a shared microwave line. Credit: RIKEN
Silicon spin qubits are a promising platform for building scalable quantum computers as they are compatible with the semiconductor manufacturing technologies used to produce today's computer chips. However, they have an important weakness: silicon spin qubits typically lose accuracy when several qubits are under parallel control. To be useful, quantum computers will need to operate many qubits simultaneously without compromising the accuracy of their operations.

Now, in a study published in Nature Physics, researchers from the RIKEN Center for Emergent Matter Science and collaborators have addressed this challenge by achieving high-fidelity control of multiple silicon spin qubits operating in parallel. Using a five-qubit silicon array controlled through a single shared microwave line, they identified a key source of errors during the simultaneous operation of qubits and developed a way to mitigate its effects.

According to Leon Camenzind, a research scientist at RIKEN and co-first author who supervised the experiments, "This shows that high-fidelity control can be maintained even when several qubits are operated at the same time and represents an important step toward scaling up silicon-based quantum processors."

Unmasking the culprit behind control errors
Quantum computers process information using quantum bits, or qubits, which represent combinations of the conventional binary states 0 and 1. In silicon spin qubits, information is encoded in the spin of an electron trapped in a silicon substrate. Precisely controlling these spins is essential for carrying out quantum operations. However, applying microwave control signals to multiple qubits at once can introduce errors that reduce computational accuracy.

The researchers began their work by finding that the major source of this degradation is a phenomenon known as the AC Stark shift, which is induced by the microwave control signals. This effect changes the effective energy levels of a qubit, shifting the phase of its quantum state and making its operation less precise. When several qubits are driven simultaneously, these shifts can interfere with the intended control of each qubit.

To address this problem, the team developed a scalable mitigation protocol based on phase calibrations. "Rather than requiring an entirely separate calibration procedure for every possible combination of qubits, our method uses calibrations between pairs of qubits to compensate for the unwanted phase shifts," explains Yi-Hsien Wu, the first author who carried out the experiments as part of his Ph.D. research.

Maintaining accuracy during parallel operation
Using an optimized device design and carefully calibrated microwave control pulses, the team achieved single-qubit gate fidelities well above 99.99% on each of the five qubits when operated individually, with the best approaching 99.999%. Fidelity measures how accurately a quantum operation is performed.

Importantly, by using the protocol, they were able to achieve fidelities above 99.99% when up to three qubits were operated simultaneously. During parallel operation of all five qubits, the fidelities remained at approximately 99.9%, a level close to the practical requirements of fault-tolerant quantum computing.

The remaining degradation was attributed to drive-induced decoherence caused by the increased microwave power needed for simultaneous operation. Unlike the phase shifts addressed by the new calibration protocol, this effect involves a loss of quantum coherence. According to Camenzind, "This is a limitation, but further improvements to device design could help mitigate this source of error."

Toward scalable silicon quantum processors
The results demonstrate that high-fidelity control need not be sacrificed when silicon spin qubits operate in parallel. "By addressing a key source of control errors through pairwise calibration," says Camenzind, "our study supports the use of shared control lines as a practical approach to scaling up quantum processors. Combined with silicon's compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing, we believe that this advance could help pave the way toward larger quantum processors capable of performing increasingly complex computations."

"Our long-term goal is to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer based on silicon spin qubits, and reliable parallel control is an essential step toward that goal," adds Seigo Tarucha, project manager of a silicon quantum computing project under JST's Moonshot Goal 6.

Most normal matter in the universe isn't found in planets, stars or galaxies – an astronomer explains where it's distributed
Story by Chris Impey • 9mo • 5 min read

This article was originally published at The Conversation. The publication contributed the article to Space.com's Expert Voices: Op-Ed & Insights.

If you look across space with a telescope, you'll see countless galaxies, most of which host large central black holes, billions of stars and their attendant planets. The universe teems with huge, spectacular objects, and it might seem like these massive objects should hold most of the universe's matter.

But the Big Bang theory predicts that about 5% of the universe's contents should be atoms made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Most of those atoms cannot be found in stars and galaxies – a discrepancy that has puzzled astronomers.

If not in visible stars and galaxies, the most likely hiding place for the matter is in the dark space between galaxies. While space is often referred to as a vacuum, it isn't completely empty. Individual particles and atoms are dispersed throughout the space between stars and galaxies, forming a dark, filamentary network called the "cosmic web."

Throughout my career as an astronomer, I've studied this cosmic web, and I know how difficult it is to account for the matter spread throughout space.

In a study published in June 2025, a team of scientists used a unique radio technique to complete the census of normal matter in the universe.

The census of normal matter
The most obvious place to look for normal matter is in the form of stars. Gravity gathers stars together into galaxies, and astronomers can count galaxies throughout the observable universe.

The census comes to several hundred billion galaxies, each made of several hundred billion stars. The numbers are uncertain because many stars lurk outside of galaxies. That's an estimated 1023 stars in the universe, or hundreds of times more than the number of sand grains on all of Earth's beaches. There are an estimated 1082 atoms in the universe.

Expand article logo  Continue reading

However, this prodigious number falls far short of accounting for all the matter predicted by the Big Bang. Careful accounting indicates that stars contain only 0.5% of the matter in the universe. Ten times more atoms are presumably floating freely in space. Just 0.03% of the matter is elements other than hydrogen and helium, including carbon and all the building blocks of life.

Scientists solved the 150-year-old mystery of why most of your molecules are right-handed
Story by Elizabeth Rayne • 4mo • 4 min read


The shape of life’s building blocks is the result of biology, chemistry, and quantum physics colliding.
The shape of life’s building blocks is the result of biology, chemistry, and quantum physics colliding.
© wildpixel - Getty Images
Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Chirality is a property that makes it impossible to superimpose an object perfectly on its mirror image, and chiral molecules are known as enantiomers.
Enantiomers that favor one “hand” or the other tend to dominate in the ingredients of life, but why that’s so has largely remained a mystery.
Now, scientists think that the spin of electrons inside a molecule makes the right- or left-handed version more efficient, which may have impacted the formation of RNA billions of years ago.
Hold your hands out in front of you, both palms facing the same way. For most people, it would be safe to say that your hands are pretty good mirror images of each other—identical, but flipped over a central vertical axis. Now, move your hands together, without flipping them, so that one rests on top of the other. While your middle three fingers might line up pretty well, your pinkies and thumbs are not going to have anything to perfectly map to. Even though your hands are mirror images of each other, when you superimpose them one over the other, they don’t perfectly line up. This property is called chirality, and in addition to making it possible for you to make weird-looking shadow puppets, it’s often observed in quantum physics.

Specifically, chirality pops up a lot in the molecules in your body, which tend to be right-handed or left-handed. In fact, most biological molecules are chiral, and the two chemically identical forms of a molecule that look like reflections are known as enantiomers. For over a century and a half, the reason why organisms have an unspoken preference for one or the other enantiomer—a phenomenon known as homochirality—has been confounding scientists. Seeking an answer, physicists Yossi Paltiel (from Hebrew University in Israel) and Ron Namaan (from the Weissmann Institute in Israel) led a research team that investigated why life consistently chooses one mirror molecule over the other.

“Over the years, several schemes have been proposed regarding the emergence of chirality in life, but none of them have explained a specific handedness,” the team wrote in a study recently published in the journal Science Advances. “On the basis of the identical energetics of the two enantiomers and symmetry-imposed mapping of their properties, it has been generally expected that the absolute value of any physical effect will be the same for the two enantiomers.”

To try and solve this conundrum, the team focused on an effect discovered decades ago—the chirality-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect—which partially explains handedness in biomolecules. The CISS effect makes it seem as if electron motion through both enantiomers of a molecule would be the same, because it’s technically the same in every other way. As it turns out, however, the ways electrons spin through the enantiomers are often similar, but they’re not exactly symmetrical. And because the spin of an electron in one enantiomer may make it work more efficiently, it keeps appearing in living things over and over again.

The direction in which electrons spin within a molecule depends on their angular momentum (momentum of rotation, as opposed to moving forwards or backwards). The research team figured out that projecting the path of these electrons involves spin-orbit coupling—an interaction inside an atom that links an electron’s spin with its rotational momentum as it orbits the nucleus—and this spin is what causes enantiomers to act differently in certain biological processes. Despite sharing the same energy, enantiomers that are in motion or functioning as transporters behave slightly differently because of opposite electron spins. Usually, it turns out that one spin direction (and thus one enantiomer) performs more efficiently than another, which is why an organism uses that version. Amino acids, for instance, are almost exclusively left-handed, while sugars tend to be right-handed.

A magnetic rock has two poles (north and south), and depending on which pole faces upward, it will attract and hold one version of a chiral molecule on its surface while leaving the other version free. There's a strange slice of Planet Earth deep below the Pacific Ocean near Easter Island which may explain the East Pacific Rise, the world's fastest spreading ocean ridge, according to scientists.

There are ancient ocean slabs at this site hiding deep in the planet's interior which could be contributing to the spread and dates back to the time when dinosaurs roamed the planet.

University of Maryland geologist Jingchuan Wang and his team have been able to work this out using seismic data, reports Science Alert.


 (Pexels)
(Pexels)
The scientists managed to identify an odd blob of mantle moving unexpectedly slow beneath the Nazca Plate that borders South America's continental plate by sending bouncing sound waves deep into the ground to form seismic maps.

The mantle makes most of Earth's volume and is made up of heated silicate rocks which are sandwiched together between a cool, thin outer crust and the core.

This layer flows in cycles over tens of millions of years due to the extreme difference in temperature at opposite edges with denser, cooler material drawn into the warmer interior.

This is called subduction and the Nazca Plate is currently subducting beneath South America, recycling surface material back into the mantle.

"This thickened area is like a fossilised fingerprint of an ancient piece of seafloor that subducted into the Earth approximately 250 million years ago.

"It's giving us a glimpse into Earth's past that we've never had before."

Wang and other scientists are improving their models of how tectonic plates have moved throughout Earth's history.

Quantum network test checks entanglement without having to trust detectors

Quantum networks are systems that connect different users or devices using principles of quantum mechanics. In the future, these networks could help improve the security of communications.

For entanglement-based quantum networks to work, however, researchers need to be able to verify that particles are entangled and assess the extent of their entanglement. Entanglement is a quantum mechanical effect in which the shared states of distant particles cannot be described as separate or independent.

Researchers at Shandong University recently introduced a new approach for verifying entanglement in a quantum network that does not require trusting the equipment used to analyze and detect photons (i.e., light particles), as most previously developed strategies do.

Their proposed method, outlined in a paper published in Physical Review Letters, was successfully used to verify entanglement in a quantum network with four users.

"Building a quantum network is only part of the challenge," He Lu, senior author of the paper, told Phys.org. "We also need to know whether the entanglement distributed across the network is genuinely there. My interest in this problem dates back to 2014, when I participated in one of the first experimental demonstrations of measurement-device-independent (MDI) entanglement witnessing."

In their earlier work, Lu and his colleagues demonstrated that conventional entanglement verification techniques could be misled by measurements that were tampered with. Specifically, manipulating measurement results could trick conventional tests into detecting entanglement when none was present.

They also tested an approach dubbed MDI entanglement witnessing that overcame this limitation. Despite its potential, this approach required the preparation of additional photons in known states for testing, which complicated its implementation.

"In recent years, our group has been developing broadband entangled-photon sources based on integrated lithium-niobate photonics," said Lu. "Meanwhile, fully connected quantum networks have been advancing rapidly, allowing entanglement to be distributed among multiple users. These developments motivated us to revisit MDI entanglement verification in the context of quantum networks and explore whether it could be implemented more efficiently."

Checking links between users in a quantum network
The main goal of the team's recent study was to develop a new practical approach for reliably verifying and quantifying entanglement across a multiuser quantum network. Most importantly, they wanted this approach to work even without trusting measurement devices or preparing additional photons for testing.

"We built our quantum network using a small photonic chip that generates pairs of entangled photons over a broad range of wavelengths," said Lu. "By distributing entangled photon pairs through different wavelength channels, we connected four users so that every pair could share entanglement. This created six connections in total, each spanning 20 km (12 miles) of optical fiber."

The key idea behind the researchers' measurement method is to use two different properties of the same photon. The researchers used the possible arrival times of photons to carry entanglement and the orientation of the photons' light waves to prepare known states for testing it. Notably, this method still requires these known test states to be prepared reliably.

"By performing a joint measurement on these two properties of each photon, we could verify entanglement without trusting the measurement devices or requiring additional ancillary photons," said Lu.

"We then recorded photon coincidence counts between pairs of users. By analyzing these measurements, we could verify the presence of entanglement in all six connections and establish a lower bound on the amount of entanglement shared between each pair of users, without relying on trusted measurement devices."

In addition to testing entanglement between individual pairs of photons, the team's approach can be applied across an entire quantum network. In initial tests, the researchers successfully used it to confirm entanglement across six links connecting four users in a quantum network, without relying on trusted measurement devices.

"More importantly, our method goes beyond simply determining whether entanglement exists," said Lu. "It also allows us to quantify entanglement by establishing a lower bound on the amount shared between users, using the same measurement data. This provides more information about the quantum resources available in the network."

The team's MDI approach also requires fewer experimental resources. This is because it uses the same photons both to realize entanglement and to verify it.

"In our earlier experiment in 2014, our implementation of MDI entanglement verification required additional ancillary photons, making the experiment technically demanding," said Lu. "In the present work, we eliminate this requirement by encoding trusted quantum inputs in the polarization of the same photons that carry time-bin entanglement."

Verifying entanglement with fewer resources
Lu and his colleagues think that their approach could make the reliable characterization of entanglement more practical for larger quantum networks. In larger fully connected networks, the number of connections between users increases, so minimizing the complexity of the methods used to verify entanglement becomes increasingly important.

"Our next goal is to extend our approach to larger and more complex quantum networks," said Lu. "In particular, we are interested in exploring both high-dimensional and multipartite entanglement. Time-bin encoding offers a natural way to generate high-dimensional quantum states, while multipartite entanglement would allow several users to share a common entangled state, opening new possibilities for quantum communication and networking."

The lithium-niobate source used by the team in their experiments produces photons with a broad range of wavelengths. This allows them to share entanglement via several separate light channels, which could be useful when creating quantum networks with more users and connections.

"As these networks become more complex, developing efficient and reliable methods to verify and quantify entanglement will be increasingly important," added Lu. "Ultimately, we hope to combine broadband integrated photon sources with scalable entanglement characterization methods to support more advanced quantum networks."

This new theory on how AI 'thinks' could impact whether it kills us all
Story by tdickerson@businessinsider.com (Truman Dickerson) • 18h • 3 min read


AI neurons and their connections are easier to understand than we thought, according to a researcher's new findings. : Sergi Reboredo/VW Pics/Universal Images Group via Getty Images
AI neurons and their connections are easier to understand than we thought, according to a researcher's new findings. : Sergi Reboredo/VW Pics/Universal Images Group via Getty Images

AI researchers don't really know how the models they are building "think."
They know what goes into them and what comes out, but that's about it.
A new theory may change that, allowing safety researchers to prevent them from going off the rails.
How AI models think may be easier to decipher than we thought.

An independent researcher who formerly worked at OpenAI published a report on Thursday that some in the AI world are describing as a bombshell. It could have implications as safety researchers scramble to get the jump on AI models that may not have humanity's best interests in mind.

Previously, the prevailing wisdom was that AI "neurons," which, like those in a human brain, light up when an AI model encounters specific stimuli, were ultimately uninterpretable — meaning, basically, that there was no way to guess why a specific neuron would fire when an AI model was exposed to, say, a picture of a cat.

Those building AI models at places like OpenAI and Anthropic don't write instructions for everything a model does. Instead, they train neurons to respond to information and influence one another. As the AI learns, it develops its own ways of processing that information, and humans have not yet cracked the code of how it works.

That means those building AI models know what goes into the AI and what comes out, but not necessarily what happens in between. So if an AI goes rogue, they don't always know why.

The author of the new report, however, says he may have discovered patterns in how the neurons react to stimuli that make sense. Nick Cammarata, who said he left OpenAI in 2023, said AI neural networks are knowable, so long as you look carefully.

Expand article logo  Continue reading

In one example, he says that a single AI neuron might react to both a cat and the front of a car. Humans would see those as two very different things, and different neurons would fire as a result. The AI, however, sees something related, perhaps the triangle shape of the two, and a single neuron fires for both.

For years, AI researchers had assumed that AI models packed unrelated things into a single neuron to save space.

"Almost the entire network is interpretable if we look closely enough," Cammarata wrote.

Hypernova vs. supernova: What's the difference?
Supernovas occur when massive stars run out of fuel for nuclear fusion, effectively ending their lives. This results in the star's core collapsing, sending shockwaves rippling through its outer layers, causing explosions we call supernovas.

Hypernovas are proposed to occur in a similar way, but for an even more massive and rapidly spinning star. This results in a vastly more energetic explosion.

When supernovas or hypernovas happen, they fling elements that the dying star has forged throughout its lifetime into their surrounding cosmic environments. These elements eventually become the building blocks of the next generation of stars.

That means analyzing the chemical signatures of stars can tell stellar scientists about their family histories.

Such elements could also be created when two massive and dense stellar remnants called neutron stars collide and merge. However, this star doesn't have certain other heavy elements that would be present after a neutron star collision.

This team considered the origins of this star using a model of the Milky Way that has been rapidly enriched with elements heavier than hydrogen and helium, which astronomers call metals, via the death of the first generation of stars.

The rapid speed at which the star spins and the fact it exists at the outskirts of the Milky Way led the team to conclude that this star escaped a metal-poor dwarf galaxy and was eventually dragged into our galaxy. The event that created it, they concluded, was the combination of a neutron star merger and an ordinary supernova.

No hypernova required. An asymmetrical supernova would be enough, the scientists found.

The team's research was published as two papers in the journal Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.

A black hole could spit itself out of its own hair, leaving a boson star in its wake

An illustration of photon paths around a black hole. (Nicolle R. Fuller/NSF)

If anything is forever in this Universe, it's black holes – or so their physics suggests.

These ultra-dense objects are possessed of inexorable gravitational fields. Matter goes in. Very little ever comes out. They punctuate the cosmos like full stops, steadily growing ever bigger, with next to nothing that can undermine their inevitability.

But that doesn't mean black holes are inviolable. And theoretical physicists have just found a method of black hole decay that's even wackier than the objects themselves.

It's possible, according to José Ferreira, Carlos Herdeiro, Eugen Radu, and Miguel Zilhão of the University of Aveiro in Portugal, that a black hole can squirt itself out of its own hair – leaving a fully functional boson star in its wake.

"Perhaps the most remarkable result is that we found a new way in which a hairy black hole can decay," the researchers told ScienceAlert.

"In one of the scenarios we call ' fission', neither the black hole nor its surrounding scalar structure is destroyed. Instead, the composite object separates into two independently viable objects: a black hole without hair and a self-gravitating lump of scalar matter: a boson star."

OK, so there's a lot going on here.

Let's start with the black holes. Although they're some of the most extreme objects in physics, under standard general relativity, black holes are actually fairly straightforward – once they're settled and doing their thing, remarkably few parameters are required to describe their behavior – spin and mass, for example.

But that doesn't mean those parameters are all there is. Actually, we don't know what other parameters might be lurking in black-hole space – but math allows physicists to explore some really weird possibilities.

These additional features are referred to as "hair", and one type of this hair can take the form of a scalar field.

Physicists have good reasons for conjuring up such strange configurations. Black holes offer some of the most extreme laboratories in the Universe, potentially allowing researchers to probe forms of matter and fields that are difficult to detect by other means.

If a black hole could support some kind of exotic "hair", studying it could therefore offer clues about particles or fields beyond those we currently know.

A scalar field can be thought of as an invisible medium that permeates space. In particle terms, it can also be described as a collection of particles with no spin.

Under some models, that field can coalesce into something like a cloud around a black hole – although not necessarily a wispy one. It can account for a substantial fraction of the entire system's mass, producing a powerful gravitational field of its own.

"Normally one might expect such matter simply to fall into the black hole. In these solutions, however, there is a particular resonance mechanism that allows the scalar field and the black-hole horizon to remain in equilibrium," the researchers said.

"The important open question was whether that equilibrium was actually stable once general perturbations were allowed. Our simulations show that, for the configurations we studied, it is not."

Initial simulations suggested that these hairy black holes could be stable – but those simulations had imposed spherical symmetry.

Although the black hole existed in three dimensions, anything that happened in one direction was required to happen identically in every other direction. This makes the calculations much simpler, but prevents the system from becoming lopsided.

Later work that allowed non-spherical dynamics revealed an instability in one model. Ferreira and his colleagues wanted to find out whether that instability was a quirk of the particular model, or a more widespread feature of these hairy black holes – and what ultimately happened as the instability grew.

They used numerical simulations to track several different configurations of hairy black holes over time, allowing the systems to evolve differently in different directions.

But there's one more piece of the puzzle. Under the right conditions, the scalar field around the black hole can hold itself together via its own gravity, forming a hypothetical, horizonless, transparent object known as a boson star.

So you can think of the system as two components bound together, but capable of existing independently.

Their results split into two possible outcomes.


YouTube Thumbnail
YouTube Thumbnail
The first, which the researchers call "absorption", is probably what you'd expect. The instability causes the delicate equilibrium maintaining the hairy black hole to collapse. The black hole devours the surrounding scalar field, leaving behind a hairless – or "bald" – black hole.

The second scenario, which the researchers called "fission", is where things got super weird.

"The black hole and the scalar configuration are held together by a competition between gravitational attraction and electromagnetic repulsion. In the fission regime, the symmetric configuration in which the black hole sits exactly at the center of the boson star is an unstable equilibrium," the researchers explained.

"A useful analogy is a ball balanced at the very top of a mountain. It can remain there if it is placed in exactly the right position, but the slightest push makes it move away."

And this is what they observed. A tiny disturbance was enough to nudge the black hole off-center. Rather than returning to equilibrium, it moved progressively farther away – eventually popping out of the scalar field like a comedone.

The field stayed together, living on as a boson star, while the black hole, now bald, went its own way.


YouTube Thumbnail
YouTube Thumbnail
Which fate awaited a hairy black hole seemed to depend, at least partly, on how compact its scalar hair was. More compact configurations tended towards absorption, while looser ones were more susceptible to fission.

This tendency held across two different scalar potentials.

That doesn't mean that black holes are currently popping out of boson stars willy-nilly across the Universe right now. For one thing, boson stars are still very much filed under "hypothetical".

For another, the researchers caution that this particular setup is primarily a toy model, rather than a realistic model of astrophysical black holes.

"The mechanism relies on ordinary electric charge, whereas astrophysical black holes are expected to carry very little net electric charge," they told ScienceAlert.

"So we would not claim that the particular objects simulated here are likely astrophysical objects. Rather, the mechanism we uncovered may point towards a broader phenomenon that could also occur in more realistic models."

One possibility the researchers want to investigate is whether rotation could play a similar role to electric charge in other models of hairy black holes.

And if an astrophysically plausible version of this instability does exist, it may not be entirely invisible.

"A violent symmetry-breaking process of this type should generate a characteristic gravitational-wave signal. In the fission case in particular, the dynamics are very different from familiar processes such as the merger of two black holes, so one would expect the waveform to carry distinctive features," the researchers explained.

"Determining exactly what those waveforms look like, and whether they could realistically be detected and distinguished from other sources, requires further dedicated study. We regard that as a particularly interesting direction for future work."

The research has been published in Physical Review D.

Under just the right conditions, information swallowed by one black hole could emerge from the other.

This idea is still very much ensconced in the realm of theory – but it has inspired a particular approach to quantum teleportation that, researchers have now discovered, doesn't actually require one of quantum computing's more powerful resources to work remarkably well.

That resource is quantum magic – and by showing that a black hole-inspired teleportation protocol can achieve high fidelity without it, physicists Sudhanva Joshi and Sunil Kumar Mishra of the Indian Institute of Technology (Banaras Hindu University) have opened a new window into understanding this very peculiar, but very real phenomenon.

Quantum teleportation doesn't work quite like the teleportation of science fiction.

No physical matter is moved, nor is the process instantaneous. Instead, the quantum state of one particle is transferred to another, using a connection between them known as quantum entanglement, as well as classical communication.

The process doesn't allow information to travel faster than light, and the original quantum state is destroyed in the process. But still, the result is almost magical: a quantum state can be reconstructed across large distances, and no one has to go anywhere.

In quantum mechanics, though, "magic" has another, more precise meaning.

In quantum computing, magic is a property that allows certain quantum operations to go beyond what can be efficiently simulated by an ordinary computer.

Think of it like freight. A truck and a plane can both transport cargo between two cities, and for some journeys, either will do the job.

But a plane has capabilities that a truck cannot efficiently reproduce, such as crossing an ocean in a matter of hours.

Quantum magic is a little like that additional capability. It allows quantum computers to perform certain calculations that ordinary computers cannot efficiently replicate – but not every quantum task requires it.

Now we need to go back to the black holes.

These ultradense objects are thought to be the fastest scramblers of quantum information in the Universe. What falls in becomes so hopelessly tangled up with everything else that's in there that recovering the original information would be extraordinarily difficult – even setting aside the small problem of the event horizon.

But according to quantum mechanics, the Universe can't just destroy quantum information. It's one of the rules. And, at least in theory, if two black holes are connected in just the right way, information that gets scrambled by one can be retrieved by the other.

That's what Joshi and Mishra set out to investigate.

To find out, the researchers mathematically investigated two different ways to scramble quantum information in a black hole-inspired teleportation protocol.

One involved a class of quantum operations known as Clifford circuits. These can generate entanglement and manipulate quantum information, but they don't require quantum magic, and their behavior can be efficiently simulated by an ordinary computer.

The other involved more general, randomly selected quantum operations, known as Haar-random scramblers, which typically possess substantial quantum magic.

The researchers crunched the numbers for both approaches, then compared how faithfully each could teleport quantum information – basically, how closely the recovered quantum state matched the original information.

And the results contained a big surprise.

On average, both approaches achieved exactly the same teleportation fidelity, even though one used quantum magic and the other didn't.

But an average is only part of the story of a dataset.

Say you have two sets of five numbers, and the average of both sets is 10. For one set, every one of the five numbers is exactly 10. The five numbers in the other set are -1,000, 0.5, 4, 45.5, and 1,000 – add them together and divide by five, and you still wind up with 10.

Something similar emerged with the two teleportation protocols. Although the average scores were the same, the Haar approach was dramatically more consistent than the Clifford approach.

In one numerical comparison involving eight quantum bits, or qubits, the variance in teleportation fidelity in the Clifford approach was around 40,000 times greater – far more spread out around its average performance.

This suggests that although quantum magic isn't necessary to achieve excellent average teleportation fidelity in this model, the choice of scrambling operations can make a rather significant difference to how consistently that fidelity is achieved.

But there was another interesting result.

The researchers also found that, as the modeled quantum systems grew larger, the Clifford approach became increasingly successful, with its teleportation fidelity approaching perfection – despite the scrambling operations generating no quantum magic at all.

Its results also became more consistent as the systems grew larger, although the Haar approach remained substantially more reliable.

The researchers also found that adding quantum magic wasn't enough on its own to improve the teleportation results. The way the scrambled information was decoded was crucial – the decoding process had to be matched to the way the information was scrambled in the first place.

Now, this doesn't mean physicists have figured out how to teleport information through actual black holes. The study is theoretical, based on mathematical models of quantum systems under idealized conditions, the kind of foundational work that could guide future attempts to reproduce and test the protocols on real quantum hardware.

Whether the findings hold in more realistic scenarios remains to be seen. But the results suggest that the ability to scramble and recover quantum information isn't necessarily dependent on the computational sophistication of the scrambling process.

That could help physicists better understand not just the wild behavior of quantum information, but a small – very small – part of what it might take to recover information from some of the most mysterious objects in the Universe.

While other The Far Side comics attempt to steal a laugh in the middle of a sigh from the reader, in this comic, Larson aims for something deeper. It's a human reaction to mock new things that are better suited to coming changes. Here, Larson humanizes the dinosaurs in this way, giving his readers a moment of perspective.

A Trophy Fit For A Man With a Gun
There Is No Victory Here

Image via Gary Larson
Image via Gary Larson
As a massive animal and nature lover, for this strip, Larson displays a hunter in successive beats that finally reveal him as a terrible person. Each moment is shown in perfect detail as a bear calmly laps at the water from a clearing, and the hunter hides behind a tree and shoots. A final panel reveals the hunter sitting in his living room by a cozy fire with the stuffed bear in an aggressive-looking pose. The posed bear is telling a story that never was, elevating the hunter’s ego as he reads a book, unfeeling and unaffected.

This comic comments heavily on the impact of recreational trophy hunting and its sad reality. While animals go about their lives placidly unaware of the dangers of humans, men with guns hide behind trees and aim dead center for their hearts, killing them without remorse. Larson perfectly illustrates the damaging and artificial glory of the infamous trophy hunters and their absurd obsession with killing animals for fun.

A Broken Line Of Birds Fly South
A Man Watches On As They Do

Image via Gary Larson
Image via Gary Larson
In comics, readers decide how long a beat can last, and in this strip’s case, it feels like it can last forever. Larson displays a flock of geese in their arrow formation as silhouettes in the sky. However, it’s quickly evident that a goose is missing because a space is left open. As readers pan down, a man watches on. However, when readers take a closer look, the horrifying realization that the man is holding the missing goose by the neck is then taken over by the realization that the man shot it.

There’s a gun tucked under his other arm, almost easy to miss, but the subtle details in this strip tell the story. The man is standing by a body of water, meaning he probably shot the goose as it rested and recharged. What is not pictured on the page is not necessary. Larson’s wonderful talent for making readers understand his best jokes without explaining himself is a feat many cartoonists struggle with. Still, Larson makes it seem effortless.

Off To A Better Place

Image via Gary Larson
Using nature to tug at the heartstrings is Larson’s specialty. In this strip, words are omitted once more as the panel shows a recently cut tree reaching two small twig-like hands to beckoning angels who also reach for it. The man who cut the young tree stands there as he watches, looking up at the events. This strip is important because it does not attempt to be funny, sarcastic, or absurd. This strip only communicates sadness.

It is a moving and slow piece that does not demand the eyes look anywhere else. But this Far Side panel demonstrates Larson’s range, which was controversial at times, and his deeply emotional connection to nature. It isn’t often that readers can see this side of The Far Side, but it is indeed very special when the comic’s many sides and layers are so wonderfully displayed at first glance.

The theory also predicts that a rotating object, such as Earth, slightly drags the surrounding spacetime as it rotates, a phenomenon known as frame-dragging.

Using superfluid helium to sense rotation
The gravitational frame-dragging effect predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity is backed by real-world observations. For instance, Earth's rotation drags the surrounding spacetime, contributing to a slow change in the direction of nearby gyroscopes' spin axes relative to distant stars, a motion called precession.

"Essentially, the Earth drags local gyroscopes along with it very slightly as it spins," explained Ellers. "One can detect this tiny frame-dragging effect in several ways. In our paper, we propose a rotation sensor, or gyrometer, based on superfluid helium."

When it is cooled below 2.17 Kelvin, about −271°C (−456°F), helium-4 (the most common form of helium) becomes a superfluid. This is essentially a liquid that can flow without friction.

The researchers propose creating a rotation sensor by filling a doughnut-shaped tube with superfluid helium-4, then detecting changes in its flow via a specially designed barrier with tiny holes or nanopores (i.e., a Josephson junction).

"The experimental signature is a quantum mechanical phase difference that appears across the Josephson junction when the device is rotating," said Ellers. "This phase difference is measured by tracking the motion of a mechanical diaphragm connected to the loop of superfluid."

Notably, similar sensors have already been demonstrated by a research team at UC Berkeley led by professor Packard and used to measure the Earth's rotation. However, the small rotational signatures associated with the Earth's frame-dragging effect have so far proved very difficult to measure with these sensors.

"The additional phase deriving from the Earth's frame-dragging effect is extremely small and its contribution can be overwhelmed by random phases due to thermal noise," explained Ellers.

"Therefore, to assess the feasibility and potential sensitivity of this experiment, we performed a novel analysis of the thermal noise properties of the proposed device. This was guided by Keith's long experience working with superfluids and what is known about their noise properties over a range of temperatures."

Toward the sensor's experimental realization and testing
This study suggests that cooling superfluid rotation sensors far below the temperature at which helium becomes a superfluid could greatly reduce disturbances caused by heat. This could ultimately allow them to precisely detect extremely small rotations, including those linked with the Earth's frame-dragging effect.

"Our results show that, based on our current best understanding of thermal noise processes in superfluid helium at temperatures well below the superfluid transition (2.17 K), a superfluid gyrometer operating at, for example, 10 mK, has the potential to match or even exceed the sensitivity of the best frame-dragging measurements to date, with significantly less measurement time," said Ellers.

"In contrast to previous measurements, our proposal also has the advantage that it could be performed in a laboratory here on Earth, rather than in space."

The researchers also calculated that changes in the helium could reveal extraordinarily tiny differences in how much time passes along different paths around the sensor's loop. This could offer another way to study how gravity affects time and test Einstein's general theory of relativity.

"For instance, one could imagine probing physical processes occurring on time scales only a few orders of magnitude larger than the Planck time, which is thought to be an important time scale in quantum gravity," said Ellers.

"Finally, another interesting feature of our proposal is that it presents an opportunity to observe a truly general relativistic effect in a macroscopic quantum system, since frame-dragging is not predicted by Newtonian gravity."

Ellers and his colleagues are now planning new studies aimed at testing their proposed sensor. To realize their sensor experimentally, they will first need to develop Josephson junctions for superfluid helium-4 that can operate at millikelvin temperatures, just thousandths of a degree above absolute zero, rather than close to the superfluid transition temperature (as was required in previous related experiments).

"Work in that direction is underway in Keith's lab at Caltech," added Ellers. "On the theoretical side, we are starting to think about what kind of new physics we could have access to if we could probe time scales as short as a few orders of magnitude above the Planck time.

"We are especially curious about whether there could be some effects deriving from quantum gravity or other beyond-Einsteinian gravitational interactions that would leave signatures in a superfluid gyrometer experiment, like the one we have proposed."

"The development of lightweight and flexible organic semiconductors, like the ones in this experiment, would be useful in electronics such as bendable displays and wearable sensors," associate professor Yasunori Matsui explained.

Light shapes compact molecular structures
An organic semiconductor can achieve optimal performance when "holes," the carriers of electrical charge, can move freely and rapidly from one molecule to another. However, to enable this flow, the molecules must be packed closely and regularly to create the most efficient pathways.

The research group led by specially appointed assistant professor Takuya Ogaki and professor Hiroshi Ikeda at the Graduate School of Engineering at Osaka Metropolitan University synthesized a new sulfur- and nitrogen-containing organic semiconductor using a photochemical reaction.

The light triggered a chemical reaction, making parts of the molecule form new carbon-carbon bonds and causing them to fold into a more compact ring structure, ideal for hole transport.

Controlling and tracing two isomers
The experiment is the latest in a series of studies on sulfur- and nitrogen-containing semiconductors. In their latest experiment, the researchers found that by changing light irradiation conditions, they could produce a mixture of two distinct molecular structures, a bent isomer (b-DNT) and a linear isomer (l-DNT), or b-DNT exclusively.

The rumor stems from news media reports saying Russia announced that a 28-year-old lab worker at the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute of Siberia had died of an "unknown" infection, and that nearly 200 people have been isolated as a result.
